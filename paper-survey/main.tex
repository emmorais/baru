\title{\bfseries Measuring Privacy-Preserving Computation in One Place:\\
MPC, FHE, ZKP and Their Hybrids}

\author{(author names elided for review)}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Privacy-preserving computation is not one field but four---secure multi-party
computation (MPC), fully homomorphic encryption (FHE), garbled circuits, and
zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP)---each with its own literature, benchmarks and
accounting conventions. A practitioner choosing between them, or a reviewer
assessing a claim that one beats another, must assemble numbers from different
papers, measured on different hardware, over different networks, counting
different things. Cross-paradigm comparison is therefore largely \emph{citation
assembly} rather than measurement.

We take a different route. We survey the paradigms through a single artifact,
\Baru{}, in which one source program runs on all of them---a plain interpreter,
three-party replicated-secret-sharing MPC, garbled circuits, BFV/BGV/TFHE
homomorphic encryption, a Binius64 SNARK, and \emph{combinations} in which
values cross between representations mid-computation without being exposed.
Every backend is driven through the same transport, charged by the same byte and
round accounting, and checked bit-exactly against the same plaintext reference.
A network cost model is validated against a real socket to within \SI{13.6}{\percent}
worst case, so a claim about a wide-area deployment is computed from measured
counts rather than asserted.

Several widely repeated intuitions survive re-measurement; several do not. Among
the results that only a single-harness study can produce: the FHE-versus-MPC
crossover is a property of \emph{the baseline's preprocessing} rather than of
either cryptosystem, and disappears once MPC is given somewhat-homomorphic
preprocessing; active security on the nonlinear surface of private inference
costs $2.63\times$ bytes but only $1.06\times$ rounds---and $9.70\times$ bytes once
the semi-honest baseline it is measured against is allowed to pack, which only the
unverified side can do. We also report our own
withdrawn claims---a ZKP backend that proved nothing, a bit-decomposition that
leaked its inputs, and a comparison-protocol crossover whose location we got wrong
three times before measuring it---because a study that reports only what worked is
not
reporting.
\end{abstract}

\section{The comparability problem}
\label{sec:intro}

If we ask how fast private inference is, the literature answers in units that
cannot be compared. Gazelle~\cite{Gazelle}, Delphi~\cite{Delphi} and
Cheetah~\cite{Cheetah} report two-party hybrid systems in which the server holds the
model in plaintext. Falcon~\cite{Falcon} reports three-party replicated sharing on a
LAN. Papers on CKKS~\cite{CKKS} report amortized throughput over batched slots, and
papers on SNARKs report prover time for a circuit that another paper would express
with a different constraint count. Each of these measurements is internally rigorous,
but taken together they do not compose. In this section we describe the obstacles that
prevent them from composing, and we state what we propose to do about it.

We remark that four obstacles recur.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Different hardware.} A $5\times$ difference between two systems
    is unfalsifiable when one ran on a server-class Xeon and the other on a
    laptop.
  \item \textbf{Different accounting.} ``Communication'' may mean payload bytes,
    serialized bytes, or bytes per party. ``Rounds'' may mean protocol rounds,
    messages, or interactive calls. These differ by more than the effects being
    measured.
  \item \textbf{Different security models.} Semi-honest and malicious figures
    appear in adjacent columns of secondary literature without the distinction
    surviving the citation.
  \item \textbf{Different functions.} Two \ReLU{} costs may be for different bit
    widths, different fixed-point scales, or---in the worst case we found in our
    own work---subtly different functions that nobody checked against a common
    reference.
\end{enumerate}

A survey that is assembled from such numbers inherits all four obstacles. The
alternative that we pursue in this work is to re-measure the paradigms inside a single
harness, in which the obstacles are controlled by construction: the same machine, the
same accounting code, machine-checked security levels, and a bit-exactness assertion
against a shared plaintext reference before any cost is quoted.

The cost of this approach is equally clear, and we state it at the outset. Our
implementations are research-quality Rust and not the tuned C++ of the systems that we
discuss, and therefore \textbf{the absolute figures given here are not competitive with
optimized implementations and should not be read as such}. What a single harness buys
us is \emph{internally valid ratios}, that is, comparisons in which the confounding
factors listed above are held fixed. It means that our internal comparisons are
stronger evidence than our external ones, and where we compare against a published
number we say so explicitly and treat the comparison accordingly.

\paragraph{Organization.} In Section~\ref{sec:scope} we delimit the scope of the
survey. In Section~\ref{sec:prelim} we give the cryptographic preliminaries, that is,
the definitions, theorems and algorithms on which the remainder depends. In
Section~\ref{sec:background} we organize the four paradigms by what each one is
expensive in. In Section~\ref{sec:artifact} we describe our artifact and the abstract
machine at its centre, and in Section~\ref{sec:paradigms} we survey and measure each
paradigm in turn. In Section~\ref{sec:related} we position this study with respect to
the literature, and in particular with respect to MP-SPDZ. In
Section~\ref{sec:method} we present the methodology, and in
Section~\ref{sec:cross} the cross-paradigm findings that motivate it. In
Section~\ref{sec:medmnist} we give a case study on private medical image diagnosis. In
Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn} we record what the methodology caught in our own work, in
Section~\ref{sec:threats} the threats to validity, and in Section~\ref{sec:open} the
problems that remain open.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{systematization} of four paradigms organized by what they
    actually cost, with the cost dimensions (rounds, bytes, compute,
    preprocessing) separated rather than collapsed into one ``performance''
    number (\cref{sec:background}).
  \item A \textbf{single-artifact measurement study}: roughly \num{70000} lines
    of Rust in which one program runs on every paradigm, with a validated
    network model and uniform accounting (\cref{sec:artifact,sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Cross-paradigm findings} not visible from any single paradigm's
    literature (\cref{sec:cross}), including two crossovers whose location
    depends on parameters the original claims left implicit---one of which we
    ourselves mislocated three times before measuring it
    (\cref{sec:crossover-nonlin}).\vspace{-1pt}
  \item A \textbf{methodology for honest benchmarking} (\cref{sec:method}), with
    the record of what it caught in our own work (\cref{sec:withdrawn}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

In this section we delimit what the survey covers, and we state the workload that we
use throughout.

\paragraph{In scope.} Semi-honest and malicious three-party MPC over rings;
garbled circuits in the honest-majority setting; RLWE-based FHE (BFV~\cite{FV},
BGV~\cite{BGV}) and TFHE~\cite{TFHE}; FHE$\leftrightarrow$MPC
scheme switching; SNARKs over 64-bit words (Binius64~\cite{Binius}); and the
supporting primitives these need---oblivious transfer, private set intersection,
and Beaver-triple~\cite{Beaver} preprocessing from both OT and
somewhat-homomorphic encryption (SHE).

\paragraph{Out of scope.} Differential privacy and federated learning (a
different threat model: they protect an \emph{aggregate}, not a computation);
trusted execution environments (hardware trust assumption); ORAM (surveyed but
not implemented, see \cref{sec:open}); and transformer nonlinearities such as
softmax and LayerNorm, which need exponential, reciprocal and inverse-square-root
subprotocols we do not have.

\paragraph{Application focus.} We use private neural-network inference as the running
workload, because it is the one application that exercises every paradigm at once. It
requires dense linear algebra, where the packing techniques of homomorphic encryption are
effective; it requires comparisons, where secret sharing and garbled circuits compete; and
it admits verifiable computation, where succinct proofs apply. We remark that some of our
results are specific to this workload, and where that is the case we say so.

\section{Cryptographic preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

In this section we give the definitions, theorems and algorithms that the rest of this
survey relies on. We present each construction in the form our artifact implements it, and
we remark where this form differs from the textbook one, because several of our results
depend precisely on those differences. In Section~\ref{sec:prelim:model} we describe the
security models and we define what hiding will mean. In Section~\ref{sec:prelim:mpc} we
present secret-sharing MPC and the three-party multiplication that needs no preprocessing.
In Section~\ref{sec:prelim:gc} we describe garbled circuits and the three optimizations
that determine their cost. In Section~\ref{sec:prelim:fhe} we give the lattice background
and the two encodings, BFV and BGV, that we implement. In
Section~\ref{sec:prelim:switch} we show how a value can cross between a ciphertext and a
sharing, and in Section~\ref{sec:prelim:zk} we briefly describe succinct proofs. For
further information on the lattice material we point the reader to our previous report on
the subject~\cite{MoraisDahab} and to the homomorphic encryption
standard~\cite{HEStandard}.

\subsection{Security models}
\label{sec:prelim:model}

A protocol among $n$ parties computes $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ revealing nothing beyond the
output. Security is defined by simulation: for every adversary in the real protocol there
exists a simulator that, given only the corrupted parties' inputs and the output, produces
an indistinguishable view. It means that whatever an adversary learns, it could have
computed by itself from what it is entitled to know. Three axes determine the cost of
achieving this.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:corruption}
An adversary is \ib{semi-honest} when it follows the protocol and tries to learn from the
messages it observes, and it is \ib{malicious} when it deviates arbitrarily from the protocol.
The \ib{corruption threshold} $t$ is the number of parties that the adversary controls; when
$t < n/2$ we say that the protocol assumes an \ib{honest majority}, and when $t \le n-1$ we say
that it tolerates a \ib{dishonest majority}. A protocol offers \ib{security with abort} when
the honest parties either output the correct value or detect the deviation and stop, and it
offers \ib{guaranteed output delivery} when they are required to finish.
\end{definition}

The distance between these settings is not only theoretical. Semi-honest protocols require
no consistency checks, and adding them is where most of the cost of active security is
spent. An honest majority admits information-theoretic techniques and keeps public-key
operations off the critical path, whereas a dishonest majority does not, which is why the
SPDZ line~\cite{SPDZ,DamgardNielsen} needs cryptographic preprocessing. Our artifact is
three-party with one corruption, which is the smallest honest-majority setting and also the
setting of ABY3~\cite{ABY3} and Falcon~\cite{Falcon}. Like them, we implement security with
abort.

We also need a precise notion of what it means for a mask to hide a value, because one of
our findings turns on it.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:hiding}
Let $a$ be a secret in $\Z_{2^\ell}$ and let $r$ be a mask drawn from a distribution over
$\Z_{2^\ell}$. The masked value $c = a + r \bmod 2^{\ell}$ hides $a$ \ib{perfectly} when the
distribution of $c$ is independent of $a$, and it hides $a$ \ib{statistically} to within
$2^{-\kappa}$ when, for every pair of secrets $a,a'$, the distributions of $a+r$ and $a'+r$
are at total variation distance at most $2^{-\kappa}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:mask}
If $r$ is uniform over the whole ring $\Z_{2^\ell}$, then $c = a+r$ is uniform and
therefore hides $a$ perfectly. If instead $r$ is uniform over $[0,2^{m})$ with $m < \ell$
and $a \in [0,2^{m})$, then the posterior distribution of $a$ given $c$ is uniform over an
interval of size $\min(c,2^m) - \max(0,c-2^m)$, which equals $1$ when $c = 0$; for uniform
$a$ the mutual information is $I(a;c) \approx 1/(2\ln 2) \approx 0.72$ bits.
\end{theorem}

The second case of Theorem~\ref{thm:mask} can be interpreted as the statement that a mask
chosen only to prevent an overflow does not hide anything in particular. We give it as a
theorem rather than as a remark because we shipped a truncation protocol that satisfied the
first requirement and violated the second, and no correctness test could observe it; we
describe the episode in Section~\ref{sec:disclosure}.

\subsection{Secret-sharing MPC}
\label{sec:prelim:mpc}

In this section we describe the sharing structures and the multiplication protocol that our
MPC layer implements. We work over the ring $\Z_{2^k}$ rather than over a prime field,
because machine arithmetic is already modular and because fixed-point truncation is more
natural there.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:sharings}
An \ib{additive sharing} of $x \in \Z_{2^k}$ among three parties is a triple
$(x_0,x_1,x_2)$ with $x_0+x_1+x_2 = x$, where party $i$ holds $x_i$. A \ib{replicated
sharing}, denominated $[x]$, gives party $i$ the pair $(x_i,x_{i+1})$, with indices taken
modulo $3$, so that every additive component is held by exactly two parties.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:rss} shows the structure. The redundancy costs storage and it buys two
properties that we use repeatedly. Linear operations remain local, since each party applies
them to both of its components. And every value that a party sends can be recomputed by
some other party, which is what makes most deviations detectable by comparison; we return to
this in the discussion of malicious security below.

Addition of two shared values is local. Multiplication is not, because the product $xy$
expands into nine cross terms $x_iy_j$ that are distributed among the parties. The classical
solution consumes a Beaver triple~\cite{Beaver}, that is, a preprocessed
$([a],[b],[ab])$ with $a,b$ random, which converts one multiplication into two openings.
Under replicated sharing with three parties this preprocessing is unnecessary, and we
consider this the single most important cost fact about the honest-majority setting.

\begin{theorem}[ABY3 multiplication~\cite{ABY3}]
\label{thm:mul}
Let $[x]$ and $[y]$ be replicated sharings over $\Z_{2^k}$, and let $\alpha_0,\alpha_1,
\alpha_2$ be a zero-sum triple, $\sum_i \alpha_i = 0$. If party $i$ computes
\[
  z_i \;=\; x_i y_i \;+\; x_i y_{i+1} \;+\; x_{i+1} y_i \;+\; \alpha_i ,
\]
then $\sum_i z_i = xy$, and $(z_0,z_1,z_2)$ is an additive sharing of $xy$ that reveals
nothing beyond it.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} Summing the three expressions collects each of the nine products $x_iy_j$
exactly once, because the index pairs $(i,i)$, $(i,i+1)$ and $(i+1,i)$ enumerate all of
$\{0,1,2\}^2$ as $i$ ranges over the three parties, and the $\alpha_i$ cancel by construction.
Moreover, each $z_i$ is masked by $\alpha_i$, which depends on a key that party $i$ does not
hold in full, hence no party learns a cross term that it should not learn. $\Box$

The zero-sum randomness is obtained without communication. Party $i$ holds PRF keys $k_i$
and $k_{i-1}$ shared with its two neighbours and computes
$\alpha_i = F_{k_i}(\mathsf{id}) - F_{k_{i-1}}(\mathsf{id})$, where $\mathsf{id}$ is a
counter that all parties advance identically; the sum telescopes to zero.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mul} states the whole protocol. We remark that its cost is one ring
element per party and one round, with no preprocessing, and that this is why an
honest-majority protocol can be cheaper online than a dishonest-majority one however
efficient the latter's preprocessing becomes.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\caption{Replicated-sharing multiplication}
\label{alg:mul}
\begin{algorithmic}
\REQUIRE The shares $(x_i,x_{i+1})$ and $(y_i,y_{i+1})$ held by party $i$; the PRF keys
$k_i$ and $k_{i-1}$; the operation counter $\mathsf{id}$. Indices are modulo $3$.
\ENSURE The replicated sharing $(z_i,z_{i+1})$ of the product $xy$.
\STATE Compute $\alpha_i = F_{k_i}(\mathsf{id}) - F_{k_{i-1}}(\mathsf{id})$, which is local
and zero-sum over the three parties.
\STATE Compute $z_i = x_iy_i + x_iy_{i+1} + x_{i+1}y_i + \alpha_i$, which is three of the
nine cross terms.
\STATE Send $z_i$ to party $i-1$ and receive $z_{i+1}$ from party $i+1$, that is, one ring
element in each direction and one round.
\RETURN $(z_i, z_{i+1})$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Crossing between arithmetic and Boolean domains.} Comparisons, $\ReLU$ and
argmax are not arithmetic circuits and they require the bits of a shared value. We denote by
$\bt{a2b}$ the bit-decomposition protocol, which takes $[x]$ to sharings of its bits, and by
$\bt{b2a}$ its inverse. Bit decomposition is a carry-propagation problem, so its round
complexity is decided by the adder: a ripple-carry adder needs depth $O(k)$, whereas a
Kogge--Stone prefix adder~\cite{KoggeStone} needs $O(\log k)$ at the price of more AND
gates. Under replicated sharing the three additive components can be re-shared as Boolean
values for free, since each one is already known to exactly the two parties that a
replicated Boolean sharing requires, and therefore decomposition reduces to a three-input
addition followed by a two-input one.

\paragraph{Malicious security by a multiplicative MAC.} As we observed above, replicated
sharing defends itself wherever a value is recomputable by another party. Auditing the
semi-honest protocol leaves exactly one point that is not, namely the value $z_i$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mul}, which depends on the pair $(x_i,x_{i+1})$ that no other party holds.
Following Chida et al.~\cite{Chida} in the ring adaptation of Eerikson et
al.~\cite{Eerikson}, we carry every wire as a pair $([x],[r\cdot x])$ under a global MAC key
$r$. Linear operations apply to both halves for free and a multiplication costs a second
multiplication, so that a batched check at the end detects any deviation except with
probability determined by the unused high bits of the ring. It means that the statistical
security of the check is $128 - \text{used bits}$, and consequently an actively secure
execution cannot also narrow the ring wire to the width of its values: the check and the
encoding compete for the same slack.

\paragraph{Fixed point and truncation.} Weights are real numbers, so a value $x$ represents
$x/2^{s}$ for a scale $s$. A product of two such values carries the scale $2^{2s}$ and must
be truncated by $s$ bits. Truncation is the awkward primitive of this area. Local truncation
of each share is cheap, introduces a one-bit error, and fails outright with small
probability. Exact truncation opens a masked value and corrects the borrow, and it is
therefore governed by Definition~\ref{def:hiding}: the mask must be wide enough for
correctness and uniform enough for privacy, which are not the same requirement.

\subsection{Garbled circuits}
\label{sec:prelim:gc}

In this section we describe Yao's construction~\cite{Yao} and the three optimizations that
determine its modern cost. We use all three, and we remark that the third one is the reason
why circuit design in this paradigm reduces to counting AND gates.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:garbling}
A \ib{garbling} of a Boolean circuit assigns to each wire $w$ two labels $W_w^0,W_w^1$,
drawn uniformly from $\{0,1\}^{\lambda}$, and to each gate a table of ciphertexts such that the
labels of the input values decrypt exactly the label of the correct output value. The
\ib{garbler} produces the tables, the \ib{evaluator} obtains one label per wire, and by
construction it cannot decide which value that label encodes.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Point-and-permute]
\label{def:pnp}
Each label carries a random \ib{pointer bit}. The pointer bits of the input labels index
the row of the gate table that must be decrypted, and therefore the evaluator performs one
decryption instead of trying all the rows.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Free-XOR~\cite{FreeXOR}]
\label{def:freexor}
Let $\Delta \in \{0,1\}^{\lambda}$ be a global offset with least significant bit $1$, and
let $W_w^1 = W_w^0 \oplus \Delta$ for every wire $w$. Then the output label of an XOR gate
is the XOR of its input labels.
\end{definition}

It means that XOR gates cost no ciphertexts and no hashing, and that negation is a
relabelling: to compute NOT it is enough to flip which of the two labels denotes zero. Only
AND gates consume bandwidth. We remark that the price of this optimization is a correlation
among the labels, since all of them differ by the same $\Delta$, and consequently the hash
used inside a gate must remain secure when its inputs are correlated; a plain
pseudorandom function is not sufficient.

\begin{theorem}[Half-gates~\cite{HalfGates}]
\label{thm:halfgates}
Under free-XOR, an AND gate can be garbled with two ciphertexts, by writing it as the XOR of a
\ib{generator half-gate}, whose value depends on a bit that the garbler knows, and an
\ib{evaluator half-gate}, whose value depends on a bit that the evaluator knows. Garbling one
AND gate costs four hash evaluations and evaluating it costs two.
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{fig:halfgate} shows the resulting cost structure of a circuit. Together with
Definition~\ref{def:freexor}, Theorem~\ref{thm:halfgates} gives the accounting we use
throughout: a circuit's communication is $2\lambda$ bits per AND gate and nothing else.

\paragraph{Three parties without oblivious transfer.} In the two-party setting the evaluator
must obtain the labels for its own input without revealing that input, which requires
oblivious transfer. With three parties and an honest majority the oblivious transfer can be
removed: two parties garble the same circuit from shared randomness and the third evaluates,
so that input labels are delivered by the agreement of the two garblers, and consistency
between the two garbled copies replaces the guarantee that oblivious transfer would provide.
The protocol that results needs one round for the entire circuit. We remark that this is the
structural reason why garbled circuits win under high latency, since the cost model is
$\text{rounds}\times\text{RTT} + \text{bytes}/\text{bandwidth}$ and this paradigm minimizes
the first term while secret sharing minimizes the second.

\subsection{Lattices and homomorphic encryption}
\label{sec:prelim:fhe}

In this section we recall the lattice definitions that we need, we state the ring learning
with errors problem, and we describe the two encodings, BFV and BGV, that our artifact
implements. We keep the lattice material to what the cost discussion requires and we point
the reader to our previous report~\cite{MoraisDahab} for the mathematical apparatus.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:lattice}
Let $B = [b_1,\dots,b_n]$ be a basis of linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The
\ib{lattice} generated by $B$ is the set $\mathcal{L}(B) = \{Bx \mid x \in \Z^n\}$, and
its \ib{fundamental domain} is the parallelepiped
$\mathcal{P}(B) = \{Bx \mid x \in [0,1)^n\}$. The determinant $\det(B)$ is independent of the
choice of basis and corresponds geometrically to the inverse of the density of lattice points.
\end{definition}

Figures~\ref{fig:lattice} and~\ref{fig:noise} illustrate the two facts about lattices that
the encryption schemes use. In Figure~\ref{fig:lattice} a point $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$
decomposes uniquely as a lattice vector $v$ plus an element $t$ of the fundamental domain, and
this decomposition can be interpreted as a modular reduction. In Figure~\ref{fig:noise} a
ciphertext is a lattice point displaced by a small error $e$, so that decryption is the
recovery of the nearest lattice point; it succeeds precisely while the error remains inside
the shaded region, in which that point is unambiguous, and it fails for the ciphertext on the
right, whose error has left it.

\begin{definition}[Ring learning with errors]
\label{def:rlwe}
Let $R = \Z[X]/(X^n+1)$ for a power of two $n$, and let $R_q = R/qR$. Given a secret
$s \in R_q$, the RLWE distribution outputs pairs $(a, as+e)$ where $a$ is uniform in $R_q$
and $e$ is drawn from a narrow error distribution. The \ib{decision} problem is to distinguish such pairs from uniform ones, and the
\ib{search} problem is to recover $s$.
\end{definition}

The hardness of Definition~\ref{def:rlwe} reduces to worst-case lattice problems, and
concrete parameters are obtained from lattice-reduction estimates~\cite{HEStandard,ABDucas}.
We remark that the security level depends jointly on $n$, on $q$ and on the error
distribution, and in particular that raising $q$ at a fixed $n$ \emph{weakens} the scheme. It
means that depth and security are coupled: a construction that needs less depth may use a
smaller modulus and is therefore secure at a smaller ring dimension.

\begin{definition}[BFV and BGV encodings]
\label{def:bfvbgv}
Let $t$ be the plaintext modulus and $\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor$. A ciphertext $c$
encrypts $m$ under $s$ with
\[
  \text{BFV~\cite{FV}:}\quad c(s) = \Delta\cdot m + e,
  \qquad\qquad
  \text{BGV~\cite{BGV}:}\quad c(s) = m + t\cdot e .
\]
\end{definition}

Everything else follows from that single line. BFV places the message in the high-order bits
and decrypts by scaling and rounding, so its tensor product carries a factor $\Delta^2$ that
must be scaled away; BGV places the message in the low-order bits and decrypts by reducing
modulo $t$, so its noise grows multiplicatively and is controlled by \emph{modulus
switching}, that is, by dividing the ciphertext modulus in order to divide the noise. We
implement both, and we did so in order to answer a specific question by substitution rather
than by argument: how much of the cost we measure belongs to RLWE itself and how much belongs
to a particular encoding.

\paragraph{Noise, depth and bootstrapping.} Every homomorphic operation enlarges the error of
Figure~\ref{fig:noise}, mildly for addition and sharply for multiplication. When the error
leaves the region where the nearest lattice point is unambiguous, decryption fails, and
therefore a parameter set supports a bounded multiplicative \emph{depth}. Gentry's
bootstrapping~\cite{Gentry} evaluates the decryption circuit homomorphically and resets the
noise, which makes unbounded depth possible and is the dominant cost of full homomorphism. It
means that the practical question for a pipeline is not how fast a bootstrap is, but whether
it occurs at all; in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid} we describe a pipeline whose depth is one by
construction and which therefore performs none.

\paragraph{Packing.} The ring $R_q$ has $n$ coefficients, so a single ciphertext carries many
values. In \emph{slot} packing the plaintext space is decomposed by the Chinese remainder
theorem and operations act component-wise, and moving data between slots requires rotations.
In \emph{coefficient} packing we exploit the fact that polynomial multiplication is a
convolution: if a vector is laid out forwards and a kernel is laid out \emph{reversed}, then
one ciphertext product places an inner product in a coefficient of the result, and with
suitable strides it places an entire matrix--vector product or convolution there. This is the
Gazelle~\cite{Gazelle} and Cheetah~\cite{Cheetah} line, and it is the reason why the cost of
our convolutions does not depend on the image size.

\paragraph{Relinearization, and how to avoid it.} A product of two ciphertexts decrypts under
$s^2$ instead of $s$, and implementations normally \emph{relinearize} back to $s$ using an
evaluation key. We remark that if the pipeline never multiplies again, then relinearization is
pure overhead, since it is enough to decrypt under the squared key. Our construction does
exactly that, and consequently evaluation keys disappear from the trusted setup and from the
network. This simplification is available only because the depth is one.

\paragraph{Threshold decryption.} When the secret key must not exist in a single place, it is
shared and decryption becomes a protocol. We implement threshold decryption, and the key it
decrypts under is generated distributively: no party holds it and no dealer produces it
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}). Both fresh and once-multiplied ciphertexts are covered, the latter
without an evaluation key, for a reason the depth-1 design supplies and which we return to
below.


\subsection{A composability theorem, and what it costs}
\label{sec:uc}

In this section we state what we can prove about composition, and we describe the two
protocol changes that were required in order to be able to state it. We remark that the
exercise was not bookkeeping: attempting the proof located a leak in $\Pi_{\mathrm{PC}}$ that
had survived every correctness test, and we describe it in Section~\ref{sec:disclosure}.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:fabb}
The \ib{arithmetic black box} $\Fabb$ over $\Z_{2^k}$ is the ideal functionality that keeps a
store of values indexed by handle and accepts the commands $\mathsf{input}$, $\mathsf{add}$,
$\mathsf{mul}$, $\mathsf{lt}$, $\mathsf{trunc}_d$ and $\mathsf{reveal}$. Only
$\mathsf{reveal}$ produces output, and the functionality leaks to the adversary the sequence
of commands together with the handles they name, and nothing else.
\end{definition}

The leakage clause of Definition~\ref{def:fabb} is the one that carries weight, and it is why
the control flow of a program matters. Our gadget library never branches on a secret, so its
command sequence is a function of the public layer shapes alone; the interpreter, in contrast,
implements $\mathsf{if}$ on a secret predicate by revealing that predicate, and therefore the
statement below is about the library and not about the language.

Figure~\ref{fig:fabb} makes two design decisions explicit, and both of them are load-bearing.

The first is the \ib{leakage clause}. The functionality tells the adversary which commands were
issued and on which handles, and therefore a protocol realizing it is allowed to have a public
control flow and nothing more. It means that a program whose sequence of operations depends on a
secret cannot be simulated, which is why Corolary~\ref{cor:gadgets} is a statement about our
gadget library, whose command sequence is a function of the layer shapes, and not about our
language, whose $\mathsf{if}$ on a secret predicate reveals that predicate.

The second is the guard on $\mathsf{trunc}_d$. We could have written truncation as a total
function on $\Z_{2^k}$, and then no protocol of ours would realize it, because our truncation is
correct only for a non-negative argument below half the ring. Writing the precondition into the
functionality instead is the honest move: the guard is exactly the bound that
Section~\ref{sec:prelim:mpc} obtains by gating before shifting, and a caller that violates it
receives $\bot$ rather than a wrong answer. We remark that this is the general shape of the ``no
free lunch'' in a UC statement --- either the protocol is strengthened to meet the functionality,
or the functionality is weakened to state what the protocol does, and the second is only
legitimate when the weakening is written down.

Figure~\ref{fig:ucworlds} recalls what has to be established. The environment $\mathcal{Z}$
chooses the inputs, sees the outputs, and talks to the adversary throughout; the protocol
realizes the functionality when for every $\mathcal{A}$ there is a simulator $\mathcal{S}$ such
that no $\mathcal{Z}$ distinguishes its interaction with the real protocol from its interaction
with $\Fabb^k$. We remark that $\mathcal{Z}$ is what makes the statement composable and also
what makes it demanding: since $\mathcal{Z}$ may run other sessions and feed their results back,
a simulator is not allowed to rewind, and any randomness that a protocol reuses across sessions
is randomness that $\mathcal{Z}$ can arrange to collide.

\begin{theorem}[Simulator]
\label{thm:sim}
Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a static semi-honest adversary corrupting $P_c$. There is a straight-line
simulator $\mathcal{S}$ that, given the leakage of Figure~\ref{fig:fabb} and the corrupted
party's inputs and outputs, produces a view computationally indistinguishable from the real
one.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} We construct $\mathcal{S}$ command by command; in every case it holds the
corrupted party's two replicated components and must produce the messages that party receives.

For $\mathsf{input}$ and for the linear commands there are no messages, since a replicated
sharing is created locally from the dealer's value and addition acts on components.

For $\mathsf{mul}$, the real protocol delivers to $P_c$ the single element
$z_{c+1} = x_{c+1}y_{c+1} + x_{c+1}y_{c+2} + x_{c+2}y_{c+1} + \alpha_{c+1}$. The term
$\alpha_{c+1}$ is $F_{k_{c+1}}(\cdot) - F_{k_{c}}(\cdot)$, and $P_c$ holds $k_c$ but not
$k_{c+1}$, so by pseudorandomness of $F$ the element is computationally indistinguishable from
uniform. $\mathcal{S}$ samples it uniformly. This is the step that needs the keys to be fresh
per session: were the same $(k,\mathsf{ctr})$ to recur in a second session that $\mathcal{Z}$
schedules, the two elements would differ by a value $\mathcal{Z}$ knows, and no uniform sample
reproduces that.

For $\mathsf{lt}$ and $\mathsf{trunc}_d$, the messages are those of
$\Pi_{\mathrm{WA}}$, $\Pi_{\mathrm{PC}}$, $\Pi_{\mathrm{SS}}$ and $\Pi_{\mathrm{Trunc}}$, and
each of their openings is covered by Lemma~\ref{lem:openings}: $\mathcal{S}$ samples the opened
value from the distribution the lemma names --- uniform on the ring, a uniform bit, or uniform
on $\Z_p^\star$ conditioned on the predicate the functionality has already given it --- and
derives the remaining messages from the corrupted party's components exactly as the protocol
does.

For $\mathsf{reveal}$, the functionality supplies the value and $\mathcal{S}$ sends the missing
component consistent with it.

Indistinguishability follows by a standard hybrid over the PRF calls, of which there are
polynomially many, replacing each in turn by a uniform value. No step rewinds $\mathcal{Z}$ or
$\mathcal{A}$. $\Box$

\begin{corolary}
\label{cor:mal}
With the MAC layer of Chida et al.~\cite{Chida} in the ring adaptation of Eerikson et
al.~\cite{Eerikson}, the same protocol realizes $\Fabb^{k,\mathrm{abort}}$ against a static
malicious adversary, provided every consistency check is deferred to one point whose outcome
depends only on whether the adversary deviated.
\end{corolary}

The proviso in Corolary~\ref{cor:mal} is not a formality. If a check can fail as a function of a
secret, then the abort itself is an output channel that $\Fabb^{k,\mathrm{abort}}$ does not
have, and no simulator reproduces it: the simulator learns from the functionality whether the
adversary cheated, and never learns the secret that a data-dependent check would consult. Our
own first attempt at this layer had exactly that defect, and we describe it in
Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn}; Brüggemann and Schneider~\cite{BrueggemannSchneider} exploit the
same shape against Trident~\cite{Trident}, Fantastic Four~\cite{FantasticFour} and Quad, and
identify a gap of this kind in the proof of SWIFT~\cite{SWIFT}, which is evidence that the
condition is easy to violate while believing one has batched a check for efficiency alone.

\paragraph{On the preprocessing.} The masks of Lemma~\ref{lem:openings} are produced by the
pseudorandom secret-sharing of Cramer, Damgård and Ishai~\cite{CramerDamgardIshai} and by
doubly-authenticated bits in the sense of Rotaru and Wood~\cite{RotaruWood}, improved by the
edaBits of Escudero et al.~\cite{EdaBits}. We remark that the functionality they realize is a
preprocessing functionality and not $\Fabb$, so the composition is two-layered: the
preprocessing realizes $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Prep}}$, and the online phase realizes $\Fabb$ in
the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Prep}}$-hybrid model. It means that our pool of
Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin} is not merely an optimization but the boundary between the
two layers of the proof, and that the miss counter which refuses to let an online phase generate
its own preprocessing is enforcing that boundary rather than only measuring it.

\begin{lemma}[Opening audit]
\label{lem:openings}
In the protocols of this artifact, every value opened to the parties is either the output of a
$\mathsf{reveal}$ command or is masked by randomness that is uniform on the domain of the
opening and used only once. Consequently each opening is simulatable from the corresponding
command's output.
\end{lemma}

\emph{Proof.} By enumeration; the sites are finitely many and Table~\ref{tab:openings} lists
them. The three interesting cases are as follows. In $\Pi_{\mathrm{WA}}$ the opened value is
$a + x$ for a preprocessed $x$ that is uniform on the whole ring, so the opening is uniform.
In $\Pi_{\mathrm{Trunc}}$ the same holds, which is the reason the mask must cover the ring and
the overflow be corrected rather than avoided. In $\Pi_{\mathrm{PC}}$ the opened value is
$m \cdot \prod_i c_i$; the $c_i$ carry no randomness of their own, hence the mask $m$ must be a
uniform unit \emph{and must remain secret}, which is what forces the zero test on $m$ to open
$m \cdot s$ rather than $m$. $\Box$

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:uc}
Assume private authenticated point-to-point channels, a pseudorandom function, and a setup
that delivers pairwise PRF keys freshly per session. Then the protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:prelim:mpc} UC-realizes $\Fabb$ over $\Z_{2^k}$ against a static adversary
corrupting at most one of three parties, semi-honestly; and with the MAC layer of Chida et
al.~\cite{Chida} in the ring adaptation of Eerikson et al.~\cite{Eerikson}, against a static
malicious adversary, with abort.
\end{theorem}

We do not claim a machine-checked proof, and we set out the argument and its hypotheses so that
the gaps are visible. Simulation is straight-line, since the simulator for a semi-honest
minority samples the corrupt party's view directly: linear operations are local, the
multiplication of Theorem~\ref{thm:mul} sends one element masked by an $\alpha_i$ the corrupt
party cannot compute, and every opening is handled by Lemma~\ref{lem:openings}. No rewinding
appears, which is what distinguishes a UC argument from a standalone one. Keys are drawn afresh
per session, so a one-time mask indexed by an operation counter is never reused across the
concurrent sessions that the UC environment may schedule; this is a property of our transport
and we state it as a hypothesis because it is not enforced by a type. For the malicious case
the abort must be simulatable, and it is: the checks are deferred to a single point whose
outcome depends on whether the adversary deviated and not on any secret. We remark that this
is exactly the property whose absence Brüggemann and Schneider~\cite{BrueggemannSchneider}
exploit against Trident, Fantastic Four and Quad, and whose specification gap they identify in
SWIFT; deferring a check hides \emph{when} an abort happens and not \emph{whether} it happens,
and only the latter is what security requires.

\begin{corolary}
\label{cor:gadgets}
Any network assembled from the gadget library, whose control flow is determined by public layer
shapes, UC-realizes the functionality that reveals only its declared output.
\end{corolary}

Figure~\ref{fig:uclayers} shows the shape of the argument, and we draw it because the layering is
what makes each step small enough to check. Reading upwards, the preprocessing realizes
$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Prep}}$ from private channels and the key setup; the online protocols
realize $\Fabb$ in the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Prep}}$-hybrid model, which is the step
Lemma~\ref{lem:openings} and Theorem~\ref{thm:sim} establish; and the gadget library realizes
$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NN}}$ in the $\Fabb$-hybrid model, which is Corolary~\ref{cor:gadgets} and
needs only that the control flow be public. It means that the boundary between the second and
third layers is the pool of Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin}: a warm server has run the bottom
layer in advance, and the miss counter that refuses to let an online phase generate its own
preprocessing is enforcing the layer boundary and not merely reporting a latency.

We remark that $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NN}}$ is the weakest of the three to specify and the easiest
to get wrong, because it is where the application decides what may be public. Ours reveals the
predicted class and the network's architecture, and nothing else: not the logits, not the
activations, and not the input. Writing it down is what makes ``the pipeline reveals only the
class index'' a checkable statement rather than a description of the code.

\paragraph{What the theorem does not cover, and what it would cost.} The hybrid pipeline is
outside $\Fabb$, because $\Fabb$ has no ciphertexts. Extending the statement to it needs three
things, and we have two. The conversion of Section~\ref{sec:prelim:switch} opens
$m + \sum_i r_i$ with each $r_i$ uniform on the plaintext space, so it satisfies
Lemma~\ref{lem:openings}. Against a malicious adversary an input ciphertext must also be shown
well-formed with bounded noise, because replication cannot detect a malformed one --- both
honest co-holders compute on it faithfully and agree --- and that proof is implemented.

Key generation was the last part of this argument to rest on a setup assumption, and it no longer
does: the key is sampled distributively, so the hybrid realizes its functionality without a dealer
rather than in a model with one. The construction follows the line Damgård and
Nielsen~\cite{DamgardNielsen} established for an arithmetic black box from threshold homomorphic
encryption and which Asharov et al.~\cite{AsharovMHE} carry to threshold FHE, with one adaptation
that the replicated setting forces and one dividend that it pays; both are described in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}. The adaptation is that components are ternary rather than uniform,
because independent sampling cannot deliver a small key and uniform shares at once, and the
resulting leak is one the standard assumption already absorbs. The dividend is that a product
ciphertext needs no evaluation key at all, since replicated shares yield a sharing of $s^2$ with no
communication.

What remains is not a setup assumption but a parameter one, and we state it in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}: the boundary proof's soundness slack is subtracted from the smudging
budget bit for bit, which leaves a smaller $\lambda$ than an honest-input analysis reports.

\subsection{Crossing between the paradigms}
\label{sec:prelim:switch}

A ciphertext and a secret sharing are two representations of the same hidden value, and the
possibility of converting between them is what makes a hybrid protocol possible. In this
section we describe the two conversions, which are illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:switch}. We remark that both are linear, and consequently neither consumes
multiplicative depth, which is the property that Section~\ref{sec:hybrid} depends on.

To pass from a ciphertext to a sharing, we add a fresh random mask homomorphically, we decrypt
the masked ciphertext, and we let the mask itself be secret-shared. The plaintext that appears
is uniform by Theorem~\ref{thm:mask}, and subtracting the shared mask leaves a sharing of the
original value. To pass from a sharing to a ciphertext, each party encrypts its own share and
the ciphertexts are added homomorphically, which is correct because the sharing is additive.

\subsection{Succinct proofs}
\label{sec:prelim:zk}

A zero-knowledge argument allows a prover to convince a verifier that it knows a witness
satisfying a public relation, revealing nothing else, and it is \emph{succinct} when the proof
size and the verification time are sublinear in the computation being proved. We remark that
these systems answer a different question from the one that MPC and homomorphic encryption
answer: they establish the \emph{integrity} of a computation performed by somebody else, and
not the privacy of a computation performed jointly. They enter this survey because a
deployment of private inference may need both properties at once. Our artifact targets
Binius64~\cite{Binius}, which operates over towers of binary fields so that operations on
64-bit words are expressed without bit-blasting them.

\section{Four paradigms, four cost structures}
\label{sec:background}

In this section we organize the four paradigms. They are usually taxonomized by the
security property that they provide, but for a practitioner who has to choose among them we
consider that the more useful axis is \emph{what each one is expensive in}, and this is the
axis of Table~\ref{tab:paradigms}. Three trade-offs follow from it directly, and they drive
most of the remainder of this survey.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
\toprule
Paradigm & Secret from & Expensive in & Cheap in & Rounds \\
\midrule
Secret-sharing MPC & computing parties & \textbf{rounds}, bandwidth & local arithmetic & $O(\text{depth})$ \\
Garbled circuits   & computing parties & \textbf{bandwidth} & rounds & $O(1)$ \\
FHE                & computing server  & \textbf{compute}, ct.\ size & rounds (none) & none \\
ZK proofs          & the verifier      & \textbf{prover compute} & verifier, proof size & none \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The paradigms organized by cost structure rather than security
property. Three trade-offs follow directly, and drive most of this paper.}
\label{tab:paradigms}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Secret sharing and garbled circuits trade rounds against bytes.} This is the
classical trade between the constructions of Yao~\cite{Yao} and of GMW, and neither of them
dominates the other. The winner depends on the round-trip time and on the bandwidth, and
therefore the location of the boundary is an empirical question. We measure it in
Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin}, where we also report the three answers that we published
before the current one.

\paragraph{Homomorphic encryption and secret sharing trade compute against
communication.} Homomorphic encryption sends much data once and then computes hard, whereas
secret sharing sends little data many times. On a fast local network secret sharing usually
wins, and on a thin pipe the balance moves. We remark that this framing omits
\emph{preprocessing}, and in Section~\ref{sec:crossover-fhe} we show that the omission is
precisely what makes most published crossovers between the two paradigms fragile.

\paragraph{Succinct proofs are orthogonal, and conflating them with the others is a
category error.} Secret sharing and homomorphic encryption keep data secret \emph{from the
parties that compute on it}. A succinct proof establishes that a computation was performed
correctly while hiding the witness \emph{from a verifier}, and the prover holds everything in
the clear. It means that a deployment which needs both confidentiality and integrity pays for
both, and that a proving time and a protocol latency do not belong in the same column. For
this reason we keep them in separate tables, and our benchmark prints the caveat next to its
numbers so that they cannot travel without it.

\subsection{The hybrid, and why it exists}

Inference in a neural network alternates linear layers with nonlinearities.
Homomorphic encryption is good at the former and bad at the latter, since a comparison is not
a low-degree polynomial; secret sharing is the reverse, since a comparison costs a bit
decomposition whereas a linear layer costs one round independently of its width.

Consequently the natural design is to evaluate the linear layers under homomorphic
encryption and the nonlinearities under secret sharing, with a \emph{scheme switch} between
them in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:prelim:switch}. This is the design that
Gazelle~\cite{Gazelle}, Delphi~\cite{Delphi} and Cheetah~\cite{Cheetah} instantiate. We
implement it and we add two elements, which we describe in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}: the
weights are encrypted as well, and the switch is used as a \emph{noise refresh}, which
removes bootstrapping from deep networks entirely.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\protect\normalfont\scshape Baru}{Baru} artifact}
\label{sec:artifact}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\protect\Baru{}}{Baru} is a CEK machine}
\label{sec:cek}

In this section we describe the abstract machine at the centre of our artifact, and we argue
that this choice is what makes the comparisons of this survey possible. Our artifact is not a
circuit compiler with a front end. It is an abstract machine for a Lisp-like language, and the
cryptography is placed behind a single interface that the machine calls.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:cek}
A \ib{CEK machine}~\cite{FelleisenFriedman} evaluates a program by rewriting a triple
$\langle C, E, K \rangle$, denominated a \ib{configuration}, where $C$ is the expression under
evaluation, $E$ is an environment that maps variables to values, and $K$ is a
\ib{continuation}, that is, a representation as data of what remains to be done once the value
of $C$ is known.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:cek} shows one step of the machine. Evaluation is a loop over a step
function: in order to evaluate an application $(f\ a)$, the machine pushes onto $K$ the
instruction ``evaluate the argument and then apply $f$'' and moves to $f$; when a value is
finally produced, the machine pops the topmost frame of $K$ and proceeds. It means that no
recursion takes place on the stack of the host language, and consequently a deep program
consumes heap rather than stack, and the entire state of the machine is an ordinary value.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0,
  reg/.style={draw,rounded corners=2pt,minimum height=7mm,minimum width=15mm,font=\small},
  lbl/.style={font=\footnotesize},
  frame/.style={draw,minimum width=17mm,minimum height=4.6mm,font=\scriptsize}]
  \node[reg] (c) at (0,0) {$C$};
  \node[reg] (e) at (1.75,0) {$E$};
  \node[reg] (k) at (3.5,0) {$K$};
  \node[lbl,below=0.6mm of c] {control};
  \node[lbl,below=0.6mm of e] {environment};
  \node[lbl,below=0.6mm of k] {continuation};
  % continuation as a stack of frames
  \node[frame] (f1) at (6.4,0.62) {apply $f$};
  \node[frame] (f2) at (6.4,0.0)  {bind $x$};
  \node[frame] (f3) at (6.4,-0.62) {$\mathsf{Halt}$};

  \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]}] (k) -- (f2);

\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{One step of the abstract machine. Because the continuation is data and not the host
stack, it can be inspected, serialized, and used to batch independent protocol calls.}
\label{fig:cek}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why an abstract machine and not a circuit.} The dominant design for secure
computation compiles a program into a circuit and then evaluates that circuit under one
protocol, as in ABY~\cite{ABY}, HyCC~\cite{HyCC}, CirC~\cite{CirC} and
Silph~\cite{Silph}. We consider that this is the correct design when the protocol is fixed,
and the incorrect one for a survey, because a circuit has already made the choices of the
protocol. A comparison compiled for garbling is a Boolean circuit, and the same comparison
compiled for arithmetic sharing is not the same object. It means that comparing two paradigms
would reduce to comparing two compilations, and any difference between the two compilations
would contaminate the measurement.

An interpreter, in contrast, keeps the program fixed and changes what a primitive
\emph{means}. Our evaluator manipulates secret values only through opaque handles and a fixed
set of operations, which is the Arithmetic Black Box of the SPDZ line~\cite{DamgardNielsen,SPDZ}.
Therefore the expression \bt{(> z 0)} is one call to \bt{lt}, and whether that call becomes a
bit decomposition, a garbled comparator or a \bt{wrap3} sign extraction is a decision taken by
a backend at run time. In other words, the same source text, the same syntax tree and the same
sequence of calls to $\Fabb$ reach every paradigm, and consequently a difference that we
measure between two paradigms is attributable to the protocol and not to the program.

\paragraph{What the continuation buys.} We use three consequences of
Definition~\ref{def:cek} in this work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-local control is data.} Condition handlers are frames on $K$, so that a
    \bt{guard} installs one and leaving its body pops it. Therefore error handling inside a
    private computation requires no exceptions of the host language, and it cannot
    accidentally unwind past a point at which a protocol must synchronize.
  \item \textbf{The abstraction admits batching, although the machine does not perform it.} A
    backend primitive receives a \emph{vector} of operands, so an entire layer of \bt{lt} calls
    can be one protocol round rather than thousands. We are precise about where that batching
    comes from, because it is not from the continuation: our evaluator issues primitives one at
    a time, and the parallelism is supplied either by a gadget that calls the batched primitive
    explicitly, or by the compiler of Section~\ref{sec:cross}, which recovers it automatically
    by levelling a dependence graph. What the abstract machine contributes is the seam that lets
    both routes exist behind one program. We remark that this distinction matters more than it
    appears to, since almost every cost result in this survey depends on the difference between
    a cost paid per element and a cost paid per call.
  \item \textbf{The state is serializable.} A configuration is an ordinary value, so an
    execution can be checkpointed, replayed, or, as we use it here, run three times in
    lockstep by three parties whose operation identifiers are required to agree. We derive
    protocol randomness from a counter that every party advances identically, and this is
    sound only because the three machines take the same steps in the same order.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The cost of this choice.} Interpretation is slower than compilation, and we make
no claim about absolute performance (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). An interpreter also cannot
perform the whole-circuit optimizations that a compiler would find, and therefore when a
compiler-based system reports fewer AND gates for the same function, that difference is real
and it is not a difference between protocols. What our design buys is not speed but
\emph{commensurability}, and in Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn} we record cases in which
commensurability is what caught an error that the numbers alone did not reveal.

\subsection{One program, many backends}

In this section we describe the interface through which the machine of
Section~\ref{sec:cek} reaches the cryptography. As we said above, our language manipulates
secret values only through opaque handles and a fixed set of primitive operations, which is
the Arithmetic Black Box abstraction of the SPDZ line~\cite{DamgardNielsen,SPDZ}: an ideal
functionality $\Fabb$ that stores values by handle and exposes
\bt{add}, \bt{mul}, \bt{lt}, \bt{share\_public} and \bt{reveal}. A backend implements those
primitives, and the evaluator never inspects a representation.

\begin{lstlisting}
; private ReLU on one neuron -- every value is secret-shared
(let ((w :sint8 -1) (x :sint8 3) (b :sint8 0))
  (let ((z (+ (* w x) b)))
    (reconstruct (* z (> z 0)))))          ; => 0

; a value computed under MPC crosses into FHE without being revealed
(let ((keys (fhe-keygen)) (x (share 6 32)) (y (share -7 32)))
  (fhe-dec keys (share->fhe keys (* x y))))   ; => -42
\end{lstlisting}

The consequence for a survey is immediate. \emph{The same source text} is the workload
for every paradigm, and therefore a comparison between paradigms cannot be confounded by two
implementations that compute subtly different functions. We remark that this is not a
hypothetical concern, since in Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn} we record a case in which it caught
exactly that.

\subsection{Composability}

Every gadget of our library, namely \bt{relu}, \bt{max2}, \bt{maxpool2d}, \bt{argmax},
\bt{dense} and \bt{conv2d}, is a pure composition of $\Fabb$ primitives: no gadget opens a value
and none branches on a secret, so the sequence of primitive calls a gadget makes is a function of
the public layer shapes. Consequently the composition theorem of the universal composability
framework~\cite{Canetti} carries security from the primitives to any network assembled from the
gadgets, and a search over architectures requires no new security argument for each architecture.
It means that the research question becomes which architecture is \emph{fastest} under the cost
model, rather than whether a given one is secure.

We remark that the hypothesis of that sentence is stronger than it looks, and we state it
properly in Section~\ref{sec:uc}. The primitives must \emph{UC-realize} $\Fabb$, which is not
implied by their being secure in a standalone sense, and every value the primitive protocols open
internally must be simulatable. Two of our own security failures were failures of precisely that
hypothesis rather than of composition, so we treat the gadget-level claim as conditional and prove
the condition separately.

\section{The paradigms, surveyed and measured}
\label{sec:paradigms}

In this section we survey and measure each paradigm in turn, in the order in which

\subsection{Secret-sharing MPC}
\label{sec:mpc}

Our MPC layer follows ABY3~\cite{ABY3}, so that $x = s_0+s_1+s_2 \bmod 2^{128}$ and party
$i$ holds the replicated pair $(s_i,s_{i+1})$ of Definition~\ref{def:sharings}. Addition and
multiplication by a scalar are local, and multiplication is the triple-free protocol of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mul}, in which each party sends one ring element to one neighbour and the
correlated randomness is regenerated locally from a single pairwise exchange of PRF keys.
There is no dealer and there is no preprocessing phase.

We remark that this layer taught us the same engineering lesson on four separate occasions,
and that the lesson generalizes beyond our artifact.

\finding{The arithmetic was never the bottleneck; the round structure was. Each
time a segment-level measurement said ``MPC is slow'', a per-phase breakdown
said ``one unbatched loop inside it is slow''.}

A batched form costs \emph{the same number of rounds for any batch width}. When we
adopted such forms throughout, the MPC segment of our convolutional network moved from
\SI{40.8}{\second} to \SI{6.1}{\second}, that is, by a factor of $6.7\times$, and the number
of messages fell from \num{29211} to \num{5591}. It means that a published MPC cost is highly
sensitive to the batching discipline of its implementation, and consequently that comparing
the round counts of two systems, without first verifying that both of them batch, compares
implementations and not protocols.

\paragraph{The cost of active security.} We measured the malicious protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:prelim:mpc} against the semi-honest one on the same workload and the same
transport, and we give the result in Table~\ref{tab:malicious}.

A second result generalizes, \textbf{with a correction we owe to
\cref{sec:crossover-nonlin}}. The statistical slack that ring-based verification
normally charges as a widening to $\Z_{2^{k+s}}$~\cite{SPDZ2k} is already
\emph{present} when the implementation shares over a ring much wider than its values:
Q16.16 values in a 128-bit ring leave \textbf{59 bits} of statistical security.

We previously added that this came at \emph{no widening cost}. That is false, and
packing the semi-honest paths is what exposed it: the 128-bit ring had been costing

$128 - \mathit{used\_bits}$, so narrowing to the value width sets $\lambda$ to zero.

revealed its price. The $2.63\times$ above is therefore a fair ratio at equal width
that \emph{understates} the cost of active security against a packed baseline. Measured:
\textbf{$9.70\times$} on the comparison workload---the semi-honest leg packs from
\num{234036} to \SI{63416}{\byte} while the verified leg is unchanged at
\num{615446}. The ratio of ratios, $3.69\times$, falls inside the packing factors we
measured on comparable paths, as predicted.

\subsection{Garbled circuits}
\label{sec:gc}

As we described in Section~\ref{sec:prelim:gc}, in the three-party honest-majority
setting the input labels are delivered by the co-garbler and no oblivious transfer is
required. Since oblivious transfer is the expensive part of two-party garbling, this changes
the economics substantially, and it is the reason why garbling is competitive in this setting
at all.

We measured the two routes head to head over the identical transport, on \num{1352}
comparisons of 20-bit values. Bit decomposition needs 9 rounds and \SI{1.29}{\mega\byte},
whereas the garbled circuit needs 1 round and \SI{1.69}{\mega\byte}. It means that
\textbf{bit decomposition is the byte-cheaper of the two}, by a factor of $1.3\times$, and
that the advantage of garbling lies entirely in the rounds: the wall clock favours it by
$18.8\times$ on a local network and by $4.0\times$ on a wide-area network with
\SI{80}{\milli\second} of round-trip time and \SI{100}{\mega\bit\per\second} of bandwidth,
the ratio shrinking as bandwidth rather than latency becomes the binding term.

\paragraph{This result reverses a figure that we published.} The same experiment
previously reported bit decomposition at \SI{5.94}{\mega\byte}, which made garbling appear
$3.5\times$ cheaper in bytes \emph{as well as} faster. That difference of $4.6\times$ was
serialisation padding: a 20-bit ring share travelled inside 128 bits, and bit decomposition is
almost entirely ring traffic whereas garbled tables are already compact. It means that the
overhead fell on one side of the comparison only. Once the encoding was corrected, as we
describe in Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin}, the ordering in bytes inverts and only the
advantage in rounds survives. We remark that both routes remained bit-exact against the
plaintext reference throughout, and consequently no correctness test could have revealed the
problem.

\paragraph{A correction to another of our claims.} The figures given above are for the
\emph{garbling primitive in isolation}. The deployable three-party protocol is smaller, once
we include the $Y2A$ return leg that converts the Boolean output back into an arithmetic
share: it is $7.7\times$ on a local network, falling to $1.8\times$ at \SI{80}{\milli\second}
and \SI{100}{\mega\bit\per\second}, and between $3.9\times$ and $2.4\times$ for the fused
$\ReLU\circ\text{rescale}$. We record both figures, because the distance between them is
exactly what is lost when a number is cited.

An earlier version of this paragraph quoted between $1.4\times$ and $1.7\times$ for the
deployable path. That figure was taken not from this experiment but from the \emph{MPC
segment} of our convolutional network, which is a different benchmark on a different workload,
and it was presented as though it corrected this one. That segment measures $6.1\times$ today.
The old figure was correct when it was written, and it became stale for a reason that we record
in Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn}: an unbatched scheme-switch import was then 94\% of the MPC
segment, which loaded both routes equally and therefore compressed the ratio toward one. We
consider that both corrections illustrate a single rule, namely that one should quote the
deployable protocol, on the workload about which the claim is being made, and name the
benchmark that produced the number.

\subsection{Fully homomorphic encryption}
\label{sec:fhe}

We use the cheapest homomorphic primitive that exists, namely a single
ciphertext-by-ciphertext multiplication, with no relinearization keys, no evaluation keys and
no bootstrapping, decrypting the resulting degree-two product directly under $s^2$ as we
described in Section~\ref{sec:prelim:fhe}. Our parameters for BFV are $n=4096$, $q=2^{100}$
and $t=2^{20}$, which lie inside the ceiling of approximately $109$ bits on $\log q$ for
$128$-bit security at that ring dimension~\cite{HEStandard}.

We consider that two results about packing are worth surveying.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reversed-coefficient packing} turns an entire matrix--vector product into
    one multiplication. We find that it is \emph{already optimal for dense layers}, since a
    product of degree $n$ admits at most $n/\mathrm{in}$ separable outputs, and therefore
    $\lceil \mathrm{out}\cdot\mathrm{in}/n\rceil$ multiplications is the limit. The SIMD
    packing of Gazelle~\cite{Gazelle} matches this throughput, but it requires rotations and
    Galois keys, and consequently it forfeits the property of needing no evaluation keys.
  \item \textbf{Convolution packing} in the manner of Cheetah~\cite{Cheetah} turns an
    entire output channel into one multiplication, independently of the size of the image. We
    measured the two layouts against each other at $28\times28$, and \num{1352}
    multiplications become \textbf{two}, while the time spent in homomorphic evaluation falls
    from \SI{77.5}{\second} to \SI{264}{\milli\second}.
\end{itemize}

\finding{BGV is indistinguishable from BFV at identical parameters (repeated runs
put the gap at $+0.5\%$, $+0.5\%$, $-0.1\%$). Where the message sits in the
ciphertext, high bits or low, is not where the cost is.}

On the arithmetic side, we use a multi-modular number-theoretic transform, in which
NTT-friendly primes are employed as a computational device and the result is reconstructed by
the Chinese remainder theorem, because a power-of-two modulus $q$ admits no direct transform.
After we moved its butterfly to Shoup-preconditioned multiplication, it became $1.8\times$
faster than NTL together with GMP~\cite{NTL} at every size that we measured. We remark that
we retain NTL as a \emph{differential oracle} rather than as a dependency, and that this is
what made it safe to rewrite the hottest code of the repository.

\subsection{The hybrid and the no-bootstrap result}
\label{sec:hybrid}

In this section we describe our hybrid construction and the consequence that we consider
its main result. The central observation is that the scheme switch which we perform in order
to evaluate a $\ReLU$ \emph{also refreshes the ciphertext}. When we switch back in the
direction of Section~\ref{sec:prelim:switch}, each party encrypts its share component afresh
and the evaluator adds the ciphertexts, and a sum of fresh encryptions is itself a fresh
encryption. It means that the noise budget of Figure~\ref{fig:noise} is reset at every
boundary between layers. Consequently a network of arbitrary depth runs with \textbf{zero

We consider that two of our implementation findings generalize to anybody who builds
arithmetic circuits over 64-bit words. The first is that comparison gates are
\textbf{unsigned}, and therefore a signed comparison must flip the sign bits beforehand;
without that step $\ReLU(-1)$ returns $-1$, which is to say that the circuit is perfectly
sound about the wrong function. The second finding is sharper, and we state it as follows.

\finding{Division cannot be checked the obvious way. A circuit cannot divide, so
the prover supplies quotient and remainder as witnesses; but checking $a = bq+r$
with $|r|<|b|$ is \emph{unsound}, because circuit multiplication wraps mod
$2^{64}$. For $a=1, b=3$ a cheating prover sets $r=0$ and $q=3^{-1} \bmod 2^{64}$
and both checks pass. Requiring the 128-bit product's high word to be the sign
extension of the low word closes it.}

We remark that we drive that forgery through the real prover as a test, so that the
soundness of the repair is demonstrated and not merely asserted.

\section{Related work, and where this study sits}
\label{sec:related}

In this section we position our work with respect to the literature. We remark that
organizing this literature by primitive would reproduce Section~\ref{sec:prelim}, and
therefore we organize it instead by \emph{what a system is for}, because in our view that is
what determines whether the numbers of one system can be compared with those of another.

\subsection{Frameworks that run many protocols}
\label{sec:related:frameworks}

\paragraph{MP-SPDZ~\cite{MPSPDZ}} is the closest system to this one in ambition and the
natural point of comparison. It implements dozens of protocols --- dishonest and honest
majority, fields and rings, semi-honest and malicious, with the SPDZ~\cite{SPDZ},
SPDZ2k~\cite{SPDZ2k}, Semi2k and LowGear~\cite{Overdrive} families among them --- behind a
Python-like front end that compiles to a bytecode executed by a virtual machine. It is more
complete than \Baru{} on the MPC axis by a wide margin, it is the source of the
triple-preprocessing figures in \cref{tab:triples}, and any practitioner choosing an MPC
protocol should start there rather than here.

We consider that three differences matter for a survey, and we emphasize that none of
them is a claim of superiority.

The first difference is the \textbf{scope of the paradigm}. MP-SPDZ is a framework for
secret-sharing MPC, in which somewhat-homomorphic encryption appears as a means of generating
triples. Our survey requires homomorphic encryption as an evaluation paradigm in its own
right, because the question of whether a given layer should be computed under homomorphic
encryption or under secret sharing has no answer inside a system in which one of the two
alternatives is only ever preprocessing.

The second difference is \textbf{compilation against interpretation}. A compiler is obliged to
commit to a protocol in order to emit code, whereas an interpreter can leave the choice to a
backend and consequently hold the program fixed across the paradigms, as we argued in
Section~\ref{sec:cek}.

The third difference is \textbf{maturity of the optimizer}, and here we were until recently
describing our own artifact inaccurately. We had written that MP-SPDZ optimizes protocol
performance while we optimize the comparability of measurements. The second half is true and the
first half understates what we have: our compiler carries an instruction graph, an as-soon-as-
possible levelling pass that collapses independent operations into one batched round, common
subexpression elimination, reassociation of dependent reductions, per-protocol cost models, and
an assignment pass that chooses a protocol per kind of operation. Those are the same categories
of pass that make MP-SPDZ's compiler effective, and Table~\ref{tab:handvscompiled} measures what
they buy against an expert baseline.

\finding{Automatic scheduling reaches \emph{parity} with the expert, and the metric that shows it

We should be precise about what ``the same number of times'' is doing in that sentence, because we
were not at first, and we corrected ourselves twice. Waits are \emph{almost} deterministic, not
deterministic. The mechanism is visible in the instrumentation: a receive is counted as a wait only
when it actually polls the transport, and one that finds its message already delivered does not, so
the quantity is the number of times a party \emph{had} to stop, and that depends on how the three
threads interleave.

Our first correction was to withdraw the phrase ``exact parity'', which we had written on the
strength of two equal readings of \num{33}. Four repetitions showed the expert's route reading
\num{32} or \num{33} while every other row held. Our second was to withdraw the accompanying claim
that only that one row was unstable: a later run that swept a second round-trip time moved three
rows by one count each. The network profile perturbs the interleaving, so the jitter is a property
of the counter and not of one route. Every wait figure we report should be read as $\pm 1$.

What survives is the argument, because none of it rests on a single count. Parity is parity within
the counter's resolution, and the effects we measure against it --- a factor of \num{136} for
levelling, \num{2.7} for the protocol assignment, \num{214} for the local-cost constant --- are not
one-count effects. We record the two corrections because the temptation each time was to quote the
equality we had happened to observe.

The reason is that a primitive call is not a round. A \bt{mul\_batch} blocks once; a
\bt{lt\_batch} performs an entire bit decomposition and blocks once per stage of the prefix
adder. Counting calls therefore weighs a comparison and a multiplication equally, and a route
that trades many multiplications for few comparisons looks better than it is. We had written
this section twice on the strength of the call count, once claiming the compiler beat the expert
and once attributing the residual to executor overhead; both readings were artifacts of the
counter, and neither survived adding one that counts blocking.

We read the corrected table as good news of a narrower kind. Automatic levelling over a
dependence graph, with no hint from the source, recovers what a careful author achieves by
remembering to call the batched primitive --- which is the premise the rest of this section needs.
It also shows which pass does the work: levelling alone leaves the compiled route at $\num{43}$
waits against the expert's $\num{33}$, and it is reassociating the dependent reduction that
closes the gap to $\num{32}$. Common subexpression elimination contributes nothing to waits at
all, and a great deal to bytes ($\num{881}$ against \SI{33.5}{\kilo\byte}), which is why the
ladder is reported per pass rather than as one number.

We also record a place where the model and the executor are defined differently, together with
what that costs, because the second part is easy to assume and was not what we assumed. The model
charges a level the \emph{maximum} over the primitives it contains, on the reasoning that a level
is one logical round; the executor issues one batched call per \emph{kind} per level and each
blocks, so a level holding a multiplication and a comparison pays their \emph{sum}. We expected
this to be the largest item outstanding. Counting the mixed levels in the network of
Table~\ref{tab:handvscompiled} shows there are \emph{none}: the schedule has \num{8} interactive
levels and issues \num{8} batched calls. The reason is structural rather than lucky --- a
$\ReLU$ written as $z \cdot (z > 0)$ puts its comparison and its multiplication at consecutive
levels by construction, and so does a mux-style $\max$ --- so the two definitions differ and this
class of program does not pay for the difference. We keep the counter (\bt{ExecStats::mixed\_levels})
so that a schedule which does pay is visible rather than inferred, and we do not claim the
optimisation.

ratio it distorts is exactly what a ranking does read. It makes garbled circuits look
$2.5\times$ cheaper than bit decomposition where the marginal figure is $7.8\times$.

This is the same slope-against-intercept error we corrected in the analytic model's local-work
constant, in a different instrument, and we record it as a pattern rather than as two incidents:
\ib{a cost measured at one scale and applied at another is an intercept masquerading as a slope},
and the symptom is that the fixed part is common while the ratio is not.

We then made the correction, and it was wrong. Substituting the marginal figures for
\bt{local\_secs} while leaving the byte term alone flipped the selection to Falcon on a thin pipe,
where measurement says garbled circuits win --- caught by the validation test rather than by
inspection. The error was making one term marginal and not the other, and the byte term does not
decompose the same way: Falcon's traffic includes \emph{its own} preprocessing, which is a
protocol-specific cost and not a fixed one common to the candidates. There is no common term to
strip. We reverted to validated totals and kept the marginal measurement as a statement about
direction: every correction to the ratio favours the champion already selected, so the assignment
is more right than the registry's numbers imply and never less.

We keep the link-keyed machinery and withdraw the example. The machinery is the right shape, the
byte-against-blocking trade it expresses is one we measure at the level of whole routes
(\S\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin}), and the assignment it selects is worth \numrange{2.75}{3.0} times in blocking, the range being the counter's own $\pm 1$.
What we no longer claim is that the comparison gadget is where the champion flips: it is not, and
the test that asserted a flip now asserts the measured fact, so a change that produces one will
fail it and be looked at.

and reports a \emph{compute} bottleneck when that term dominates. Its problem was narrower and
worse: the constant feeding it had carried the comment \emph{``an estimate; calibrated in P1''}
since it was written, and that calibration never happened. Our own calibration command validates
predicted rounds and predicted online bytes against a measured run and reports zero error on both;
it never touched compute. So we fitted it, and we describe the method because the number is
machine-specific and the method is not: wall clock on a free network is per-lane work \emph{plus} a

fixed part to every lane. We measure four batch widths from \num{1024} to \num{65536} and fit the

We labour the control because this benchmark's own history is three readings that compared
quantities which were not comparable, and the test that fixes the fourth now asserts that the
mismatched comparison is the flattering one --- so the reading that would overstate the compiler
is pinned in place as a thing to avoid rather than described in a comment. What the result does
establish is the premise the next section needs: the comparison backends differ enough to be
worth choosing between, the choice is invisible to correctness, and the compiler is a good enough
consumer of such a choice that it will not squander it.

We remark that this is a narrower claim than MP-SPDZ's, and deliberately so. MP-SPDZ implements
tens of protocols against our handful, its compiler vectorizes where ours does not, and its
engineering is years ahead; a practitioner choosing an MPC protocol should still start there.
What Table~\ref{tab:handvscompiled} supports is only that automatic scheduling over a dependence
graph recovers, and slightly exceeds, what a careful author achieves by remembering to call the
batched primitive --- which is the premise the rest of this section needs, because a per-operation
protocol assignment is worth deriving only if the code that consumes the assignment is not itself
leaving rounds on the table.

\paragraph{Frameworks based on circuit compilation.} ABY~\cite{ABY} introduced
mixed-protocol two-party computation with explicit conversions between arithmetic, Boolean and
Yao sharings. HyCC~\cite{HyCC} automated the decision of \emph{partitioning} a program,
compiling ANSI C into a mixed-protocol program. CirC~\cite{CirC} unified circuit compilation
across MPC and proof back ends, and Silph~\cite{Silph} treats the assignment of protocols as an
optimization problem. Our own cost model, which we describe in Section~\ref{sec:cross}, is a
small instance of this line, and we report there that the hybrid split for a neural layer can be
\emph{derived} from measured per-operation costs rather than annotated by hand. We remark that
the distinction from those systems is again interpretation: they choose an assignment and emit a
circuit, whereas we choose per call and keep the program.

\subsection{Three-party honest-majority protocols}
\label{sec:related:3pc}

\paragraph{ABY3.} The work of Mohassel and Rindal~\cite{ABY3} is the protocol layer that our
artifact reimplements, namely replicated sharing over rings, the triple-free multiplication of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mul}, conversions among arithmetic, Boolean and Yao representations, and
fixed-point primitives for machine learning. For our purposes its central insight is that with
three parties and one corruption the \emph{online} cost of a multiplication falls to one ring
element per party and requires no preprocessing at all, and it means that honest-majority secret
sharing becomes competitive with homomorphic encryption on linear algebra.

\paragraph{Falcon.} On that base, Wagh et al.~\cite{Falcon} construct a set of protocols for
the nonlinearities, and we reproduce them in detail: private comparison over a small prime field
$\Z_p$, the \bt{wrap3} computation of the wrap of three summands, $\Pi_{\mathrm{DReLU}}$ and
$\Pi_{\mathrm{ReLU}}$, the share selection $\Pi_{\mathrm{SS}}$, max-pooling with a one-hot
indicator, and exact truncation. Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin} is largely a reproduction
study of these protocols, and we consider it the part of this survey in which reproduction
produced the most, because matching a published protocol at its own ring width and on its own
network obliged us to separate the offline cost from the online one, and that separation changed
our conclusion twice.

We flag two details for anybody who reproduces this work. The first is that the $\ReLU$ of
Falcon carries a one-hot indicator because Falcon also \emph{trains}, and consequently charging
an inference-only pipeline for that indicator is not a fair comparison. The second is that its
exact truncation states a bound on the mask which is necessary for correctness and insufficient
for privacy, in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:hiding}; we describe the consequence in
Section~\ref{sec:disclosure}.

\paragraph{Other work in the honest-majority setting.} SecureNN~\cite{SecureNN} preceded
Falcon with three-party protocols that avoid garbled circuits for comparisons.
ASTRA~\cite{ASTRA} and the works of Chida et al.~\cite{Chida} and of Eerikson et
al.~\cite{Eerikson} supply the machinery for malicious security that we described in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim:mpc}, and SING~\cite{SING} continues the thread of nonlinearity
protocols. We implement the MAC construction of Chida and Eerikson and we measure its cost
against the semi-honest baseline rather than assuming it.

\subsection{FHE-based and hybrid inference}
\label{sec:related:fhe}

The space of hybrid designs is where the largest differences that we measure in this survey
are found. Gazelle~\cite{Gazelle} established the pattern, namely linear layers under lattice-based
homomorphic encryption with packing, nonlinearities under garbled circuits, and conversions between
the two. Delphi~\cite{Delphi} moved work into an offline phase and traded accuracy for speed by
means of polynomial activations, and MiniONN~\cite{MiniONN} had taken an earlier mixed approach.
CrypTFlow2~\cite{CrypTFlow2} contributed protocols for comparison and truncation with substantially
better constants, and it is the reference point toward which our own findings repeatedly point.
Cheetah~\cite{Cheetah} contributed the coefficient-packed convolution of
Section~\ref{sec:prelim:fhe}, which requires no rotations, and it is the technique whose exactness
we verify at $5\times5$ in Section~\ref{sec:medmnist}. We remark that PEGASUS~\cite{PEGASUS} and
CHIMERA~\cite{CHIMERA} address switching \emph{between homomorphic schemes} rather than between
homomorphic encryption and secret sharing, which is a different problem from ours.

Our contribution relative to this line is not a faster hybrid. It is that the hybrid route,
the pure homomorphic route, the pure secret-sharing route and the garbled route are all present in
a single artifact over a single transport. It means that the question of what the hybrid actually
buys, and of where the crossover lies, can be answered by measurement rather than by comparing
numbers taken from four papers with four experimental setups.

\subsection{Homomorphic encryption schemes and libraries}
\label{sec:related:he}

BFV~\cite{FV} and BGV~\cite{BGV} are the two schemes that we implement, in the encodings of
Definition~\ref{def:bfvbgv}. CKKS~\cite{CKKS} targets approximate arithmetic over the reals and we
do not use it here, because our results depend on bit-exactness against a plaintext reference.
TFHE~\cite{TFHE,TFHErs} targets fast Boolean bootstrapping and is present as a backend for
comparison. SEAL~\cite{SEAL} and NTL~\cite{NTL} are the reference implementations against which any
work of this kind is measured, and we make no claim of competing with them on constants, as we
state in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. Our selection of parameters follows the standard for
homomorphic encryption~\cite{HEStandard} and the line of work on lattice
estimators~\cite{ABDucas,DucasvanWoerden,KirchnerFouque}.

We remark that our one negative result in this area is a scheme that we \emph{removed}. It was an
NTRU variant in the style of LTV and YASHE~\cite{LTV,YASHE}, it was fast, and at the parameters that
our construction requires it was not secure; we describe the episode in
Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn}.

\subsection{What is distinctive here}
\label{sec:related:delta}

No individual protocol in our artifact is novel, and we claim none. We believe, however,
that three elements are unusual in combination.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Four paradigms behind one abstraction, in one artifact and over one
    transport}, with a single program text reaching all of them, as we argued in
    Section~\ref{sec:cek}. The existing frameworks are deep in one family of paradigms and they
    treat the others as preprocessing or omit them entirely.
  \item \textbf{Comparability treated as the object of study.} The uniform accounting, the
    bit-exactness gate and the validated cost model for the network are themselves the
    contribution, and we remark that several of our findings are corrections to comparisons
    rather than new speedups; we present them in Sections~\ref{sec:method}
    and~\ref{sec:withdrawn}.
  \item \textbf{Negative and withdrawn results kept in the record}, with the component deleted
    in the cases where it lost. We report three optimizations that were measured and rejected,
    four successive wrong answers to a single question about a crossover, and two gaps in the
    security of our own code, one of which we created while implementing a published protocol
    faithfully enough to inherit an under-specified precondition.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

In this section we present our methodology. It is the part of this work that we would most
like other artifacts to adopt, and consequently we state each element as a rule together with the
error that the rule exists to prevent.

\subsection{Identical transport, uniform accounting}

Every protocol, whether arithmetic secret sharing, garbled circuits, the Falcon
reproduction or the hybrid, runs over the same transport stack, which is wrapped in counting and
latency decorators that observe the traffic without touching the code of the protocol. It means
that both legs of any comparison pay a real serialization, a real passing of messages and a real
counting of bytes, and consequently there is no asymmetry in which one side is simulated and the
other is not.

We remark that this matters more than it appears to. Our in-process simulator \emph{is not the
protocol}, since its multiplication of shares reconstructs and reshares locally, which is free and
insecure, and it understated the pure secret-sharing baseline by approximately $2.7\times$,
namely \SI{6.7}{\milli\second} simulated against \SI{18}{\milli\second} real. Every number that we
report for secret sharing in this survey is the real wire protocol, with three party threads that
exchange serialized messages.

\subsection{A validated network model}
\label{sec:netmodel}

Our cost model charges $\text{rounds} \times \text{RTT} + \text{bytes}/\text{bandwidth}$,
which allows a benchmark to move the parties between continents without deploying anything. We
\textbf{validated it against a real loopback TCP socket}, and we measured a worst-case error of
\SI{13.6}{\percent} and an error below \SI{1}{\percent} through the middle of both sweeps, with the
constants of the link measured rather than assumed. It means that the cost really is linear in the
rounds and linear in the bytes, with no missing cross-term, and this is what licenses us to compute
a wall-clock figure from measured counts.

Constructing this validation revealed two defects in our harness that had been silently
distorting results. The per-message latency timers could \emph{reorder} back-to-back messages, and
the bandwidth model did not serialize bulk transfers cumulatively, which \emph{undercharged
precisely the byte-heavy phases} that the crossover experiments are about. Both defects now have
regression tests. We mention them because a cost model that has never been checked against a socket
should be assumed to contain defects of this kind.

We state the limitation openly: the constants are those of loopback, with a round-trip time of
\SI{23}{\micro\second}, and not those of a wide-area network, and therefore a wide-area deployment
requires its own two constants.

\subsection{Bit-exactness as a gate on quoting cost}

\finding{A cost comparison between routes computing different things is
meaningless.}

Consequently we check every backend against a common plaintext reference before any number
is reported, and our benchmarks refuse to present a clean table when a cell disagrees. The encrypted
convolutional network reproduces the plaintext prediction on 600 of 600 embedded medical images, and
we ran it twice, before and after two optimizations, with both passes agreeing on all 600 images. It
means that those optimizations are \emph{exact} and not merely close.

\subsection{Machine-checked security attestations}
\label{sec:attest}

Every engine attests the security level that it actually implements, in a single auditable
table, and asking a session for more than an engine attests is an \emph{error rather than a silent
downgrade}. We remark that exactly one of our engines attests malicious security.
The editing rule is that an attestation records what the code \emph{does}, never
what the roadmap intends: raising one is the last step of an upgrade, after the
cheating-detection tests pass.

We consider this the most transferable idea in the artifact. Security level is
the dimension most often lost when numbers move between papers, and making it a
machine-checked property of running code---rather than prose in a limitations
section---is cheap to implement and hard to get wrong by accident.

\subsection{Recording withdrawn results---and deleting the component}
\label{sec:withdrawn-policy}

We keep negative, superseded and withdrawn results in the experimental record beside the
successful ones, together with the reasoning that produced them, and we summarize what this
practice caught in Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn}.

We remark that the half of the rule which is easy to omit is the other one: \textbf{when a
measurement condemns a component, the code is removed and the number is retained}. An artifact
that accumulates every paradigm it has ever measured becomes unmaintainable, and worse, it
becomes misleading, because a reader assumes that everything present is recommended. On this
basis we removed the following components. Single-server private information retrieval, which
loses to downloading the whole database by approximately $13\times$ and admits \emph{no}
crossover in the database size $N$. The SEAL and CKKS backends, whose approximate comparison
costs seconds per operation and carries a real error, against milliseconds and bit-exactness
under secret sharing, and which carried \SI{3.4}{\mega\byte} of vendored C++. An NTL polynomial
backend, which our hand-written transform beat by $1.8\times$ at every size. And an unsound
placeholder for the proof system, which we describe in Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn}.

Two consequences follow. The first is that \textbf{the figures given here for a removed
component are not reproducible from the current artifact}, and our experimental record says so at
the point of measurement. The second is that where a removed measurement still feeds a live claim,
it survives as the smallest object that can carry it. In other words, the bootstrap counts
attributed to a levelled competitor in Table~\ref{tab:deep} come from the level arithmetic of the
CKKS sign approximation, which is now a module of forty lines with no dependencies, and whose
figures are pinned by a test precisely because the apparatus that produced them has been
deleted.

\section{Cross-paradigm findings}
\label{sec:cross}

In this section we present the findings that motivated the single-harness approach, in the
sense that the literature of no single paradigm could have produced them. In
Section~\ref{sec:crossover-fhe} we show that the crossover between homomorphic encryption and
secret sharing belongs to the preprocessing of the baseline. In
Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin} we locate the crossover between the two routes for the
nonlinearities, and we report the four answers that we gave before the present one.

\subsection{The FHE-versus-MPC crossover belongs to the baseline's preprocessing}

ciphertext is flat. Our cost model predicts this behaviour and our measurements confirm it, but

dishonest majority with preprocessing from oblivious transfer, homomorphic encryption takes it
beyond approximately $256$ multiplications. We consider that this explains a structural fact
about the literature which is otherwise puzzling, namely that \emph{every hybrid inference
system combining homomorphic encryption and secret sharing is two-party}. Gazelle, Delphi and
Cheetah are situated precisely in the regime in which the model says that the hybrid pays.

\subsection{Protocol assignment can be derived, not annotated}

Given the measured costs of each backend, a search over the assignments of protocols to
kinds of operation returns $\bt{mul}\to\text{fhe}$ and $\bt{cmp-lt}\to\text{binary}$ for an
encrypted layer of width 256 containing a comparison. In other words, the search \emph{derives}
the architecture of Gazelle, Delphi and Cheetah instead of receiving it as an annotation. We
remark that the prior work on the assignment of protocols, from HyCC~\cite{HyCC} through
Silph~\cite{Silph} to SING~\cite{SING}, is \textbf{restricted to secret sharing}, and that
extending the cost model so that it spans secret sharing \emph{and} homomorphic encryption is
what makes the hybrid architecture an output of the search rather than an input to it.

Two structural non-linearities make this objective non-separable, which is worth
noting for anyone tempted to formulate it as an integer linear program: a
dependence level costs the \emph{maximum} over its instructions, not their sum;
and conversions \emph{batch per level}, so a crossing's cost depends on whether
another crossing already happens there.

We must qualify the byte figure, because we quoted it for some time without the qualification and
it changes how decisive that axis is. The party-to-party transport is not all the traffic: the
hybrid also moves FHE ciphertexts and scheme-switch payloads on the \ib{evaluator link}, which does
not pass through the instrumented party transport and so appeared in no counter, while the latency
column paid its bandwidth cost throughout. Fitting a diagnosis's time against the reciprocal of
bandwidth exposed the gap --- the fit is clean and linear, with a slope near \SI{29}{\mega\byte}
where the traffic table said \SI{10.1}{\mega\byte} --- and the missing term is
\SI{17.0}{\mega\byte} per diagnosis, identical for both routes because it is the same pipeline
either way. Total traffic is therefore \SI{27.0}{\mega\byte} for garbled circuits against
\SI{20.4}{\mega\byte} for Falcon.

\finding{Falcon's byte advantage is $1.33\times$ on what the deployment actually moves, not the

dilutes it by $2.2\times$. Because the term is common, the crossover \emph{location} is unchanged:
adding the same quantity to both curves cannot move where they cross. What changes is the
magnitude of the advantage, and hence how strongly bandwidth ought to be expected to favour one
route.}

single draw per segment batches better than many inline ones, and since its bytes are unchanged,
warming the server improves both terms at once. Falcon's message count is no longer a fixed
number, and we give it as a range above for that reason: closing the private-comparison leak of
\S\ref{sec:uc} replaced an opening of the mask with an opening of a masked product, and the
rejection sampling that needs adds a data-dependent number of retries. Two consecutive runs of the
identical configuration measured \num{474} and \num{482}.

\paragraph{We reported three other answers before this one, and we consider the sequence to
be the more useful result.} Each of them was reproducible, each computed the correct diagnosis, and
each agreed with our cost model; the first even matched a prediction of the break-even point at
\num{17.1} against the $15$ to $20$ that we measured.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{$15$--$20$\,\si{\mega\bit\per\second}.} Falcon's
    $\Pi_{\mathrm{WA}}$ preprocessing was dealt from a shared seed, free precisely
    because it was not secret---so this compared Falcon's \emph{online} cost with
    garbled circuits' \emph{total}.
  \item \textbf{No crossover at any bandwidth.} With a real offline phase Falcon
    measured \SI{32.5}{\mega\byte} against \SI{10.1}{\mega\byte} and appeared dearer
    in both terms. \textbf{82\% of those bytes were padding}: \Baru{} serialised ring
    shares as \bt{u128} whatever their width.
  \item \textbf{A tie.} The grid stopped at \SI{10}{\mega\bit\per\second}, above the
    boundary.
\end{enumerate}

We remark that the first answer was wrong in \emph{both} directions at once, since dealt
preprocessing flattered Falcon whereas the padding penalised it. Correcting both errors moves the
boundary \emph{upward}, and consequently Falcon wins over a wider region than we originally
claimed, for entirely different reasons. In other words, the two errors had partly cancelled, and
this is why the figure appeared sound.

\textbf{Neither the bit-exactness gate nor the agreement with the cost model detects any of
the three errors.} Both of those mechanisms establish that a computation is correct and that its
cost is explicable, and neither of them asks whether two measurements are \emph{commensurable}.
What caught these errors was decomposing each total into the terms that the model charges, namely
the phases, the messages, the bytes and the padded share of the bytes, and then verifying that
both protocols were charged for the same things.

We now apply one further rule generally: a spot check is not evidence about an encoding.
Narrowing the ring wire to a fixed width of 21 bits was exact for the dataset whose activations fit
in $2^{20}$, and it was wrong for \emph{every} image of the dataset whose activations reach
$2^{22}$, because the safe width is a property of the model and not a constant.

\paragraph{What we can still say.} The architectural claim survives and is worth
separating from the performance one:

\finding{Three implementations of one comparison, bit-exact against each other
behind a single interface, make the protocol backing every \ReLU{}, max-pool and
argmax a one-line choice rather than a rewrite---which is what made this error
detectable rather than shippable.}

\paragraph{The per-deployment claim, withdrawn and then restored by measurement.} We
briefly concluded the opposite of what we had claimed: that there was \emph{nothing} to
choose on this workload, because garbled circuits dominated everywhere we had measured.
That conclusion rested on a limitation rather than on a result. \bt{lt\_falcon\_batch} ran
its preprocessing inline, so every figure in \cref{tab:crossover} was a \emph{total}, and
Falcon is a protocol designed to put work in a query-independent phase---charging it inline
is charging it for the thing its design exists to avoid.

Building the split was the open work, and it is now built: preprocessing is generated per
MPC segment from a plan a cold run \emph{measures} (sizes cannot be derived from the layer
shapes without duplicating the batching decisions inside max-pooling, the fused ReLU and
argmax), and a pool that fails to serve a request is a hard error rather than a silent
fallback, so an ``online'' latency cannot quietly contain preprocessing. The warm row of
\cref{tab:crossover} is the result, and it restores the per-deployment claim in a stronger
form: a \emph{cold} server should prefer garbled circuits above ${\approx}\SI{25}{\mega\bit\per\second}$,
a \emph{warm} one should prefer Falcon almost everywhere at low round-trip time, and the
gap between those two answers is a factor of four in bandwidth.

Falcon's own claim---that its \emph{online} phase is cheap---is now testable too, and it
holds. At $\ell = 32$ on Falcon's own LeNet workload our online phase is
\SI{0.25}{\mega\byte} per party against a \SI{2.87}{\mega\byte} total: \emph{below} the
\SI{0.74}{\mega\byte} they report, while our total is several times above it. Which
comparison is the fair one depends on whether their figure includes preprocessing, which we
cannot determine from the paper, so we publish both and call neither the answer.

Two asymmetries keep this honest. Garbled circuits gain nothing from warming---they have no
query-independent phase here---so the warm boundary must be read \emph{alongside} the cold
one and never instead of it; presenting only the warm row would reproduce exactly the error
that produced our first withdrawn crossover. And our byte counters are whole-run totals, so
in a warm sweep the latency column is an online cost while the traffic figures remain
totals; the two are not on the same footing and our tool says so in its own output.

\paragraph{The scope of the withdrawal.} One bit decomposition per element is an
artefact of \emph{our} construction rather than a lower bound. Writing
$w_{01} = \mathrm{wrap2}(x_0,x_1)$ and $y = (x_0+x_1) \bmod L$, one gets
$\delta = w_{01} \oplus [\,y + x_2 \geq L\,]$ (verified exhaustively at
$\ell = 6$). Party~0 holds both $x_0$ and $x_1$, so it computes $w_{01}$ and $y$
locally and for free, leaving a single comparison between a value it knows and a
value parties~1 and~2 know---a millionaires' problem, not another
$\Pi_{\mathrm{WA}}$, and one that the same no-OT three-party garbling used for the
\bt{gc} route settles in one round and ${\approx}\ell$ AND gates. We have not
implemented it and claim no cost for it. It is enough to fix the scope: what we
withdraw is \emph{our} crossover, and the correct reading of
\cref{tab:crossover} is ``\Baru{}'s Falcon loses to \Baru{}'s garbled circuits once
preprocessing is charged''---not that Falcon's DReLU fails to repay its
preprocessing, which these measurements do not show and the construction above
suggests is false.

We consider that three points deserve the attention of a privacy venue.

\paragraph{Privacy costs nothing in accuracy, and we show this rather than asserting it.}
The integer model matches its floating-point parent to within $0.2$ points, and the encrypted
pipeline reproduces the plaintext diagnosis bit-exactly on every one of the 600 images.

\paragraph{Accuracy alone misreads a medical classifier.} The test set of PneumoniaMNIST is
approximately $60\%$ positive, and our model earns its \SI{84.78}{\percent} largely by
\emph{over-calling the disease}, since it attains \SI{97.2}{\percent} of sensitivity against
\SI{64.1}{\percent} of specificity, and its balanced accuracy is \SI{80.64}{\percent}. None of
this is visible while only the accuracy is reported, and we remark that none of it is a
cryptographic result; nevertheless a paper that claims private diagnosis is obliged to report
it.

\paragraph{The ceiling on accuracy is the packing and not the cryptography.} We initially
attributed the size of the model to the noise budget of the homomorphic scheme, and that was
wrong. Three limits bind, and the noise is the third of them. Spatially, a valid convolution of
size $3\times3$ followed by pooling takes $28 \to 13 \to 5 \to 1$, which admits at most three
convolutions. By \emph{packing}, the constraint $2\cdot \mathrm{in}_c \cdot H \cdot W \lesssim n$
caps the first convolution at 12 channels, before the input of the second one exceeds $n=4096$.
Depth, in contrast, really is free, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}.

We have since lifted that limit on packing without enlarging the ring. A convolution is
linear in its input channels, and therefore a layer that is too wide for a single ciphertext can be
evaluated as equal groups of channels whose products are \emph{added}, which is free
homomorphically and requires neither an evaluation key nor any depth. We remark that doubling the
ring to $n=8192$ would also have worked, and that it would have charged every operation of the
pipeline, whereas splitting the channels charges only the layers that overrun.

\textbf{It buys accuracy on one of the two datasets, and the difference is
instructive.} Retraining both at $\mathrm{conv}(16,32)$---whose second convolution
takes 16 channels at $13\times13$, degree \num{5266} against $n=4096$, hence two
groups of 12---moves organa from \SI{81.64}{\percent} $[81.1, 82.2]$ to
\textbf{\SI{82.93}{\percent}} $[82.4, 83.5]$, disjoint intervals on \num{17778} test
images. Pneumonia does not move: \SI{84.78}{\percent} to \SI{83.97}{\percent} with
overlapping intervals, its validation accuracy rising while its test accuracy falls.
Both remain bit-exact with zero bootstraps.

So ``the packing was the accuracy ceiling'' holds \emph{conditionally}, and the
condition is data: organa's \num{34561} training images can use the extra capacity,
pneumonia's \num{4708} overfit it. Either experiment alone would have been misleading.

\section{What the methodology caught in our own work}
\label{sec:withdrawn}

In this section we record what the methodology of Section~\ref{sec:method} caught in our own
work. We consider that the credibility of a study rests partly on what its authors were willing to
withdraw, and consequently each item below is recorded in the artifact rather than quietly fixed.

\paragraph{A backend for proofs that proved nothing.} Until 2026-07-31 the \bt{verify}
routine of the shipped backend never checked that a witness satisfied any constraint, and it means
that anybody holding the public circuit could forge an accepting proof. We retain the routine,
unused, so that a soundness test can keep demonstrating precisely what it failed to do.
\textbf{Every figure for the proof system that predates that date is withdrawn.}

\paragraph{A break of privacy in bit decomposition.} Our previous \bt{a2b} masked its input
with a value derived from a \emph{public} lockstep counter, and therefore the input was recoverable
from the transcript by any observer. Everything built upon it, that is, $\ReLU$, max-pooling and
argmax, leaked its activations. We remark that the advertised property of requiring no extra
communication per bit was in fact a \emph{symptom}, since the carry chain was free precisely because
every operand was already public. Repairing the protocol cost $4.4\times$ in time and approximately
$130\times$ in bandwidth, and we later recovered both by means of the triple-free multiplication of
Theorem~\ref{thm:mul} and of batching.

\paragraph{An insecure homomorphic scheme, removed rather than caveated.} Our fast engine was
a port of a scale-invariant NTRU construction~\cite{LTV,YASHE}. An analysis showed it to be insecure
at the parameters that our construction requires, even at depth
one~\cite{ABDucas,KirchnerFouque,DucasvanWoerden}: the large LTV secret raises the fatigue point to
approximately $2^{59}$, but a single multiplication with a usable plaintext modulus $t$ forces
$q \gtrsim 2^{84}$. We deleted it rather than keeping it as a fast reference, every result
transferred bit-exactly, and the homomorphic column of our tables became slower.

\paragraph{A channel of selective failure that passes every functional test.} Attaching MACs
to the leaves of a bit decomposition by multiplication is unsound. A corrupt party injects an offset
$\delta$, the first gate downstream then carries the MAC error $\delta y$ where $y$ is a secret bit,
and consequently the execution aborts when $y=1$ and completes when $y=0$. It means that the abort
itself carries one bit of the secret.

\finding{Deferring the abort does not close this. Batching hides \emph{when} an
abort happens, not \emph{whether} it happens; those are different channels and
only the first is closed by batching. We had asserted the opposite before
building it.}

\paragraph{A crossover withdrawn three times, for three different reasons.} The
location in \cref{sec:crossover-nonlin} has been wrong three ways: measured against
dealt (hence free) preprocessing, so it compared one protocol's online cost with
another's total; then measured with Falcon inflated $3.2\times$ by serialisation
padding, from which we wrongly inferred that no crossover could exist anywhere; then
reported as a tie because the grid stopped above the boundary. It is at
$2$--$4$\,\si{\mega\bit\per\second}. \textbf{All three passed our bit-exactness gate
and agreed with our cost model}, the first even matching its break-even prediction.
A related lesson we now apply: a two-image spot check is not evidence about a wire
format---narrowing to 21 bits was exact for the $2^{20}$ dataset and wrong for
\emph{every} image of the $2^{22}$ one.

\paragraph{Comparison errors of framing.} Pricing FHE's \emph{linear} part
against MPC's \emph{whole layer} (omitting the \ReLU{} that FHE cannot do at
all); computing a crossover against a trusted-dealer baseline (asking whether FHE
beats free preprocessing); and the cross-domain triple comparison corrected in
\cref{sec:triples}.

\paragraph{Honest negatives retained.} PIR loses to downloading the database by
${\approx}13\times$ with no crossover in $N$. Hybrid-TFHE does not pay. Faster
ring arithmetic does \emph{not} bring FHE close to MPC. Per-layer bit-width
tuning was measured at ${\approx}10\%$ and not built. A fused garbled max-of-four
was measured as a $1.68\times$ \emph{loss} and not built.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

In this section we state the threats to the validity of our results.

\paragraph{Quality of the implementation.} Our code is research Rust and not tuned C++.
Consequently the absolute figures are not competitive, and the ratios measured inside the harness
are the defensible output. Where we compare against a published number, as for instance our sign
extraction at approximately \SI{0.043}{\milli\second} against the optimized C++ of Falcon, the
difference is one of implementation and not one of protocol, and we say so.

\paragraph{A single machine.} All of our figures were obtained on one Apple M1 Pro, and
therefore the variance across machines is uncharacterized. The network model is validated on
loopback, and consequently the constants for a wide-area network are modelled rather than
measured.

\paragraph{Run-to-run variance bounds how sharply a boundary can be read.} Re-running an
\emph{unchanged} configuration as a separate process moved one cell by \SI{13}{\percent}.
Repeats \emph{within} one measurement session spread under \SI{2}{\percent}, but they share a
key-generation session, run back to back and hit warm caches, so they bound jitter rather
than reproducibility---our sweep reports the median of three and marks cells whose ranges
overlap, and that marking is therefore weaker than it looks. We read every crossover as good
to about one grid step, and treat cells within ${\approx}\SI{13}{\percent}$ of each other as
unresolved regardless of which is nominally faster. Latencies carry this variance; message
and byte counts are deterministic and reproduce exactly, which is why we prefer them for any
claim that has to be sharp.

\paragraph{The simulated network is a model.} We validated it to \SI{13.6}{\percent} in the
worst case, but it remains the expression $\text{rounds}\times\text{RTT} +
\text{bytes}/\text{bandwidth}$ and not a real wide-area network with congestion, jitter or loss of
packets.

\paragraph{The disclosed cryptographic gaps, one of which has since closed.} The threshold
\emph{key generation} of our homomorphic layer was dealer-based, and we recorded it on the code
itself and in the attestation table rather than only in prose. It no longer is, and the reason the
dealer was there turns out to be worth more than its removal.

A dealer makes the key components \ib{uniform} modulo $q$ while their sum stays \ib{ternary}, by
choosing $s_2 = s - s_0 - s_1$. Both properties at once are what it buys, and no protocol in which
each party samples its own component independently can have all three of independent sampling, a
small sum, and uniform components. Sampling each $s_i$ uniformly makes $s$ uniform modulo $q$, and
BFV decryption computes $c_0 + c_1 s = \Delta m + (e_1 + e_2 s - e u)$, whose $e_2 s$ term then
exceeds $q/2t$: decryption fails outright rather than degrading.

Our key generation takes the other horn. Each party samples $s_i$ ternary and sends it to its
predecessor, so the replicated layout $(s_i, s_{i+1})$ survives --- deliberately, because
replication is what makes a partial decryption checkable by recompute-and-compare, and a protocol
producing only additive shares would remove the dealer at the cost of the malicious-security
argument. A single corruption then learns two of the three components, hence
$k = s_i + s_{i+1}$, and that $s - k$ is ternary. That is not the weakness it appears to be,
because the adversary's residual problem is the one the scheme already rests on:
\[
  b + a k \;=\; -(a(s-k) + e),
\]
an RLWE sample whose secret $s - k$ is ternary. We cannot test hardness, but we can and do test
the identity that the reduction rests on --- the measured residual has sup-norm exactly $3$, the
sum of three ternary errors --- so a broken reduction would be visibly rather than quietly vacuous.
The cost is that $\|s\|_\infty \le 3$ rather than $1$, about \num{1.6} bits of noise budget for a
fresh ciphertext and \num{3.2} for a product, which we report rather than assume because it
competes with the smudging budget.

\finding{The expensive part of threshold FHE does not arise here at all. A product decrypts under
$s^2$ because we relinearize nothing, and under replicated sharing each party forms its own share
of $s^2$ \emph{with no communication}: writing $s^2 = \sum_i s_i^2 + 2(s_0s_1 + s_1s_2 + s_2s_0)$,
party $i$ holds $(s_i, s_{i+1})$ and computes $s_i^2 + 2 s_i s_{i+1}$, and the three sum to exactly
$s^2$. It is the same identity replicated multiplication uses, which is no coincidence --- it is
the same sharing. Distributed generation of a relinearization key, the costly step in threshold
FHE, is therefore not deferred but unnecessary, and that is a dividend of the depth-1 design rather
than of the key generation.}

We measured the cost of using it before proposing to, and we separate two figures that are easy to
conflate. Against one local decryption, a threshold decryption is $3.5\times$ in \emph{total work},
which is the throughput cost; the \emph{latency} cost is one party's partial decryption at
$1.18\times$, plus one communication round, because the three partials are computed concurrently on
three machines. Quoting the first as a latency figure would repeat the error this paper records
elsewhere, so the artifact prints both with that warning and asserts that one party performs
between a fifth and a half of the total, which is the premise the parallelism argument needs.

\paragraph{What the input-boundary proof buys, and what it costs.} The boundary is the one place
where replication cannot help and zero knowledge is unavoidable, and we can now say concretely why.
Adding half a $\Delta$ to a single coefficient of an input ciphertext --- the mildest tamper that
does anything at all --- turns a computed result of \num{42} into \num{43}, and two honest
co-holders recomputing the evaluation \emph{agree} on the \num{43}, because the evaluation was
performed correctly. The fault is in the input, which no amount of recomputation inspects. We draw
attention to the off-by-one: a malformed input need not produce a value that a sanity check would
reject, and an adversary with more room than half a $\Delta$ can choose noise large enough that
whether the decryption wraps depends on the model weights, which turns a correctness failure at the
boundary into a channel that leaks what the protocol exists to protect.

The proof itself is implemented: a Schnorr-style $\Sigma$-protocol over the ring, made
non-interactive by Fiat--Shamir, with Lyubashevsky rejection sampling rather than noise flooding, so
that a released response is \emph{perfectly} hiding rather than statistically so. The interesting
part is what it costs, and it is not the prover time.

\finding{Soundness slack at the boundary is paid for out of the smudging budget, bit for bit. A
$\Sigma$-protocol extracts a witness whose norm exceeds the honest one by a slack factor, so a
verifier accepting the proof is accepting inputs with noise up to $2^{\text{slack}} \cdot B$; that
noise grows through the evaluation and arrives at the threshold decryption, where the flooding that
hides the key share must cover it. Our measured \num{26}-bit slack leaves $\lambda = 30$ at the
largest modulus this implementation can represent, against the \num{40} that an
honest-input analysis reports. The two analyses were not previously on the same footing, and the
gap is entirely this term.}

That is why the artifact's attestation for this scheme stays semi-honest: not because the proof is
missing, but because it is a tested rather than reviewed implementation, and because $\lambda = 30$
is below what we are willing to call a malicious-security claim. Both are settleable --- a tighter
proof, or a wider modulus than a \bt{u128} representation admits --- and neither is settled here.

\paragraph{What remains open.} Two things, neither cryptographic in the sense above. The first is
the review and parameter question just described. The second is harness structure: our benchmark
evaluates the homomorphic half single-threaded with one decryption key, because simulating one
evaluator is cheaper than simulating three, so the reported figures do not yet include the
threshold path even though the protocol no longer requires a party that can decrypt.

The Falcon backend's preprocessing used to be the second such gap---dealt from a
common seed, so all three parties could reconstruct the mask. It is now generated
by bit composition, at a cost of one bit decomposition per element offline. That cost is
what the two Falcon rows of \cref{tab:crossover} separate: the cold row pays it on the
critical path, the warm row hoists it. An earlier draft of this section said
\cref{tab:crossover} measured Falcon's \emph{online} cost while another paragraph said every
figure in it was a \emph{total}. Both statements were in the paper at once; the table now has
a row for each and says which is which.

\paragraph{A third gap, found while building the pure Falcon route, and closed.}
\label{sec:disclosure}
Falcon's exact truncation \emph{opens} $c = a + r$. Its stated precondition is that
$a+r$ must not wrap, which is a \emph{ceiling} on the mask, and our first
$\Pi_{\mathrm{ReLU}}$ satisfied it the cheapest way, with $r$ no wider than the value.
That is correct and hides almost nothing: the posterior on $a$ given $c$ collapses to a
point at $c=0$, and for uniform $a$ the leak is $1/(2\ln 2) \approx 0.72$ bits per
truncation, where the batched-bit-decomposition route it replaced opens no function of a
secret at all. Making the route ``purer'' had therefore traded away a security property.

No correctness test could have caught it---leakage is not a wrong answer---and all three
tests covering truncation preprocessing happened to pass a mask narrower than the ring, so
none ever exercised a wrapping one. The fix is not statistical slack but a \emph{wrap bit}:
let $r$ cover the whole ring, making the opened value uniform and \emph{perfectly}
independent of $a$, then correct the resulting overflow with $[c < r]$, which is provably
the overflow indicator and is computable from a public $c$ and a secret $r$ for one
narrowed \bt{a2b} and one $\Pi_{\mathrm{SS}}$ lift. The alternative---$\kappa$ bits of
statistical slack---needs a ring wider than our narrowed wire encoding permits and measured
$1.9\times$ \emph{dearer} in bytes than bit decomposition. The narrow-mask request is now
unrepresentable rather than merely documented, and the wrap identity is verified
exhaustively over all $(a,r)$ at $\ell = 10$.

\paragraph{A fourth gap, found by attempting the composability proof, and closed.}
\label{sec:pcdisclosure}
$\Pi_{\mathrm{PC}}$ opens $m \cdot \prod_i c_i$ and reads only whether the result is zero. The
$c_i$ carry no randomness of their own --- they are determined by the compared bits, the public
$r$ and the secret $\beta$ --- so the unit $m$ is the only thing between the transcript and the
product, and the comment on our own implementation said exactly that. Our routine for sampling
$m$ nevertheless \emph{opened} each candidate in order to reject zeros, and returned the same
shares. The justification attached to it read that opening whether a mask is zero leaks nothing,
because $m$ is drawn independently of any input and the sampling runs offline. The premise is
true and the conclusion does not follow: what the code opened was $m$ itself, and the purpose of
$m$ is to blind a \emph{later} opening, so a public $m$ blinds nothing.

The consequence is measurable rather than hypothetical. With $m$ known, $\prod_i c_i = d/m$ is
public, and at $\ell = 6$ a single predicate class contains \num{31} distinct products, so the
opening carried $\log_2 31 \approx 5$ bits where the predicate is entitled to one. In
$\Pi_{\mathrm{WA}}$ the compared secret is the wrap mask $x$ while $r = a + x$ is public, and
therefore those are bits of the activation.

The repair is to test the mask without opening it: draw a second uniform $s$ and open
$d = m\cdot s$. Then $d \neq 0$ implies $m \neq 0$, and conditioned on any accepting $d$ the
mask is still uniform on $\Z_p^\star$, because each candidate admits exactly one witness. We
verify the counting exhaustively over $\Z_p \times \Z_p$ rather than statistically. The cost is
one extra random share and one extra batched multiplication per attempt, both offline, and the
per-attempt rejection rate rises from $1/p$ to $2/p - 1/p^2$ because either factor may vanish.

We record two things about how this was found. It was not found by testing --- every correctness
test passed before and after, since leakage is not a wrong answer, and the protocol computed the
right comparison throughout. It was found by writing the enumeration that
Lemma~\ref{lem:openings} needed, which forced the question ``what distribution does the
simulator sample this opening from?'' at a site where the answer turned out to depend on a
secret. Together with the truncation of Section~\ref{sec:disclosure} that is two of our nine
openings repaired by the same question, which we take as the strongest argument we can offer for
stating a composability claim in a form precise enough to be checked.

\paragraph{Breadth of the workload.} Our findings are anchored on inference in feed-forward
convolutional networks at $28\times28$. We do not cover transformers, larger images, or workloads
other than inference.

\paragraph{This is not an adversarial evaluation.} Our tests for the detection of cheating
drive real attacks over the transport against the malicious layer, but we remark that this work is
not a security audit, and that none of our formal proofs is machine-checked.

\section{Open problems}
\label{sec:open}

In this section we list the problems that remain open, in the order in which we consider them
tractable.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A private convolutional network at the scale of CIFAR} requires SIMD packing
    in slots, since coefficient packing bounds the size of the image. We remark that the story of
    composition and security is independent of this choice.
  \item \textbf{Transformer nonlinearities} (softmax, LayerNorm) need
    exponential, reciprocal and inverse-square-root subprotocols.
  \item \textbf{Malicious security at the boundary with homomorphic encryption.} Replication
    cannot catch a \emph{malformed input ciphertext}, because both honest co-holders compute on it
    faithfully and therefore agree. It means that the positioning becomes sharper: a
    zero-knowledge proof is needed \emph{only at the input boundary and never for the evaluation},
    whereas verifiable homomorphic encryption on a single server needs one over the whole
    computation. We have since implemented the boundary proof, that is, a proof that a ciphertext
    is a well-formed encryption with bounded noise, and what remains open is a parameter question
    rather than a construction: the proof's soundness slack is subtracted from the smudging budget
    bit for bit, so the measured \num{26}-bit slack leaves $\lambda = 30$ at the widest modulus a
    \bt{u128} representation admits, against the \num{40} an honest-input analysis reports. Either
    a tighter proof or a wider modulus recovers it. Composing the proof with the malicious
    secret-sharing layer, so that a single protocol carries both guarantees, remains open
    alongside it.
  \item \textbf{Secret-index array access}, priced by the cost model, before
    ORAM. CirC~\cite{CirC} implements exactly the two cases either side of that
    line and does not implement ORAM at all.
  \item \textbf{Per-instruction protocol assignment}, versus the current
    per-operation-kind granularity.
  \item \textbf{Cross-machine and real-WAN validation} of the cost model's
    constants.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

Cross-paradigm claims about performance in privacy-preserving computation are not at present
falsifiable, because the numbers come from sources that cannot be compared. In this survey we have
described a harness in which such claims become falsifiable, and we have reported what changes when
the paradigms are re-measured under a uniform accounting.

Three of those changes are worth restating. The crossover between homomorphic encryption and secret
sharing belongs to the preprocessing of the baseline rather than to either cryptosystem. Active
security costs bytes and not rounds. And the boundary between the comparison protocols lies between
$20$ and \SI{30}{\mega\bit\per\second} for a server that pays for its preprocessing on the critical
path, and above \SI{100}{\mega\bit\per\second} for a server that has generated it in advance, which
is a difference of a factor of four in bandwidth between two deployments of the same protocol. We
mislocated that boundary four times before locating it, and each of the four figures was
reproducible and agreed with our own cost model. In Section~\ref{sec:crossover-nonlin} we report
that sequence and not only its endpoint, because the failure mode which it illustrates, namely two
measurements that are individually sound and mutually incommensurable, is not caught by any check
that we had.

We consider that the transferable part of this work is not the artifact but the discipline:
identical transports for both legs of any comparison, a cost model checked against a real socket,
bit-exactness as a precondition for quoting a cost, and the security level as a machine-checked
property of running code rather than as a sentence of prose. Each of these is cheap to adopt, and
each of them caught something here that would otherwise have been published.
