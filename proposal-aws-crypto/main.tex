\section{The architectural gap}

\heir{}~\cite{heir} is, by its own account and by community adoption, the de
facto compiler infrastructure for homomorphic encryption: an MLIR-based stack
with dialects spanning \texttt{secret}, \texttt{bgv}, \texttt{ckks},
\texttt{cggi}, library APIs for OpenFHE, Lattigo, TFHE-rs and Jaxite, and
hardware targets. We take that as given. \textbf{This proposal is not an attempt
to produce faster homomorphic code than \heir{}; inside \heir{}'s domain that
would be an engineering result, not a scientific one.}

What interests us is the boundary of that domain, which \heir{}'s own paper
draws precisely. Three consequences of a static, FHE-only lowering pipeline:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Nothing can be opened mid-computation.} A ciphertext cannot be
inspected without the key, so declared-leakage protocol design --- reveal one
bit, then branch in the clear --- is not expressible at any cost. (Obliviousness
is \emph{not} a difference: \texttt{convert-if-to-select} evaluates both arms,
and so does our own mux. We checked, and withdrew that claim; §4.)
\item \textbf{Every nonlinearity must be approximated}, and not inferred:
\heir{} lists polynomial approximation requiring user annotation for degree and
interval among its stated limitations.
\item \textbf{No interactive protocol appears anywhere in the stack.} No
dialect models secret sharing, garbled circuits, rounds, or a party.
\end{itemize}

None of these is a defect: they are the correct consequences of targeting FHE,
where a ciphertext cannot branch and a comparison is not an arithmetic circuit.
But together they fix a boundary whose cost nobody has measured, because
measuring it requires a system on both sides of it.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Thesis.} For an important class of programs --- machine-learning
inference above all --- the cheapest and the \emph{most accurate} backend
changes at points \emph{inside} the program, and the switch itself is cheap. A
compiler that can only lower a whole program to one paradigm must pay the worse
option everywhere. Representing the switch requires an execution model in which
``the rest of the computation'' is a first-class object; it is awkward in a
static SSA dataflow IR and natural in a continuation-based one.
\end{quote}

\noindent\textbf{Why ML is the right witness, not an arbitrary application.} A
convolutional network is literally an alternation of the two regimes. Linear
layers are depth-shallow, massively parallel and pack into ciphertext slots ---
the regime FHE was built for. Nonlinearities (ReLU, max-pool, argmax) are
comparisons, which FHE cannot do and must approximate, and which secret sharing
computes exactly in a few rounds. The optimal program for a CNN is therefore
neither an FHE nor an MPC program but an alternation, switching at every layer
boundary --- and the alternation is forced by the network architecture rather
than contrived by us.

\section{The architecture, and what is already built}

\baru{} is a working research compiler and runtime: one Lisp-like source
language over four backends --- plain interpreter, 3-party MPC (ABY3-style
replicated sharing, with a malicious-with-abort mode), FHE (BFV/BGV, TFHE-rs,
and a CKKS arm on OpenFHE), and ZK proofs (Binius64) --- with values crossing
between MPC shares and FHE ciphertexts mid-computation without being exposed.

Its evaluator is a defunctionalized continuation machine: an explicit
\texttt{Continuation} algebraic data type, an \texttt{Eval}/\texttt{Apply} task
trampoline over an explicit stack, and a step budget. Two properties follow, and
we distinguish sharply between what this already delivers and what it enables.

\noindent\textbf{Already delivered.} \emph{(i)} A secret-dependent conditional
is a continuation frame, and the interpreter's form \emph{opens} its condition
--- a declared-leakage branch, which an FHE-only compiler cannot express because
nothing can be opened without the key. \emph{(ii)}
Backend choice is per-operation, by dynamic dispatch through a \texttt{Backend}
trait, rather than fixed by whole-program lowering --- which makes a mid-program
scheme switch an ordinary operation, not an escape hatch. The flagship result
rests on this: the MPC round trip a ReLU already requires \emph{also}
re-randomises the FHE ciphertext, so an arbitrarily deep network runs with
\textbf{zero FHE bootstraps} on an engine supporting one multiplication.

\noindent\textbf{Enabled, and proposed here (Thrust~3).} Continuations are not
yet serialized nor used to suspend at a round boundary --- the transport blocks
synchronously. Making the continuation a value turns ``the rest of the
protocol'' into data, permitting \emph{dynamic} round scheduling: $k$
independent subcomputations interleaved on one party, each resumed as its
message arrives. The static scheduler shows the prize --- exchange count stayed
at 32 for every batch size from 1 to 8 --- but only for statically known
dependency graphs.

\section{What the boundary costs: six measured results}

Full tables in Appendix~A. All figures are measured elapsed times on one machine
with both arms checked against the same plaintext reference.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{1. The crossover is measured, and it runs both ways.} In
October 2026 we completed the \heir{} baseline described in §4 --- including the
Tier~A control, which confirms our own FHE arm is within 13\% of
\heir{}-generated OpenFHE, so what follows is about paradigms and not about our
implementation quality. Both arms evaluate the same MLP on the
same weights with $x^2$ activation --- the same function --- at
\texttt{HEStd\_128\_classic}, on one machine, each verified against the same
plaintext reference.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrl@{}}
\toprule
Matched disclosure (both return logits) & \textbf{\heir{}} & \textbf{2PC LAN} & \textbf{2PC WAN} & \\
\midrule
\texttt{64x32x10}, depth 3 & 167\,ms & \textbf{2\,ms} & 434\,ms & $2.6\times$ \heir{} \\
\texttt{64x32x32x10}, depth 5 & 659\,ms & \textbf{2\,ms} & 654\,ms & even \\
\texttt{64x32x32x32x32x10}, depth 9 & 1493\,ms & \textbf{4\,ms} & \textbf{1099\,ms} & $1.4\times$ ours \\
\texttt{64x32($\times$5)x10}, depth 10 & \textbf{SIGSEGV} & \textbf{5\,ms} & \textbf{1322\,ms} & ours runs \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{On LAN secret sharing wins at every depth, by $101\times$ to
$377\times$, and the margin widens. On WAN \heir{} leads at depth 3 by
$2.6\times$, the two are even at depth 5, and ours leads from there until \heir{}
stops running.} Our arm's own elapsed time moves $\mathbf{217\times}$ between
the two links (2\,ms to 434\,ms) while the homomorphic arm's does not move at
all --- that single fact decides the verdict, with nothing about the
cryptography changed. \emph{``Is MPC or FHE faster''} is not well-posed, and a
compiler that cannot model the difference cannot make the choice.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{2. Two thirds of the gap was disclosure policy, not protocol.}
Our arm computes the \textbf{argmax inside the protocol} and reveals only the
class index; \heir{} returns the whole logit vector and lets the client take the
maximum in the clear. The comparison had been charging us for strictly more work
\emph{and} crediting us with strictly less disclosure. Removing the private
argmax --- matching \heir{}'s disclosure --- takes wide-area exchanges from 20 to
\textbf{8} at depth 3: \textbf{the private argmax was 60\% of wide-area cost.}
It is also a guarantee \heir{} cannot offer at any price, since CKKS cannot
compare. So the defensible statement is two-sided, and we give both: at matched
disclosure the crossover is at depth 5; with the stronger guarantee it costs
$2.5\times$ and still leads \heir{} from depth 9.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{3. The boundary is a capability cliff.} At multiplicative depth 10 the \heir{} pipeline dies with \textbf{SIGSEGV
during key generation} --- at 554\,MB peak resident against the 564\,MB of the
depth-9 run that succeeds, so \emph{not} an out-of-memory kill, and with no
``cannot find secure parameters'' message either. Depth 9 completes; depth 10
does not run at all, while the secret-sharing curve continues as a straight line
($+7$\,ms per layer on LAN). We have not isolated whether the fault lies in
\heir{}'s parameter selection or in OpenFHE 1.5.1; both are candidates and both
are being reported upstream.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{4. Approximation returns the wrong answer, at depths that occur.}
This is the result that makes the thesis more than a performance argument. On a
depth sweep of LeNet-5, \textbf{the CKKS arm returned the wrong class at 13
levels} --- the reference top-two margin was 2 quanta and the worst logit error
was 15. The exact (secret-sharing) arm returned the reference class at all five
depths, including margins of one and two quanta. Approximate arithmetic
accumulates error through every rescale and key switch; stochastic truncation's
error is a carry in the last fixed-point bit, unbiased, so it does not
systematically add. Since polynomial approximation of the nonlinearity is the
\emph{only} route available to an FHE-only compiler, this is a correctness
consequence of the architectural boundary, not a tuning issue.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{5. The same regime-dependence holds for CNNs.} On LeNet-5 the
margin is $32\times$ to secret sharing on latency, ${\sim}8\times$ on throughput
at batch 8, and $885\times$ past the level budget (Appendix~A.2--A.3). A
discontinuity sits even \emph{below} the bootstrap cliff: 13 to 15 levels forces
ring $2^{15}\!\to\!2^{16}$ and cost $61.1 \to 160.0$\,s, $2.6\times$ for two
levels while multiplication count moves 1.9\% --- security parameters, not work.
A line against a staircase: that is the compiler's decision surface.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{6. The language surface determines round complexity.} The same
CNN written in \baru{} as one writes a CNN in a scalar language --- a loop over
sliding windows, a comparison per activation --- costs \textbf{507\,920
messages}. Exposing already-existing batched primitives to the \emph{language}
and fusing ReLU with rescale takes the identical computation to \textbf{33\,106
messages} and a private diagnosis from 794\,508\,ms to \textbf{911\,ms} ---
$872\times$, every integer path bit-identical. The frontend, not the protocol,
was the cost.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What this does not establish.} The CNN margins (result 4) come
from \emph{our own} CKKS arm, $15\times$ off Orion at 3.5\% slot utilisation;
only the MLP rows above are against external code. And the Lisp layer costs real
time --- the same network as a \baru{} program on the plain interpreter takes
299\,ms against 268\,ms for the hand-written Rust pipeline \emph{with image and
model encrypted}. Reducing it is Thrust~3's secondary objective.

\section{The \heir{} comparison, in three tiers}

The comparison must be designed so that the architectural claim is separable
from implementation quality on either side. We propose three tiers, and we
regard Tier~A as a precondition for reporting B or C at all.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Tier A --- Parity. \emph{Achieved for MLPs.}} The control is
that \baru{} must be within a small constant factor of \heir{}-generated OpenFHE
at matched security: \textbf{if our FHE arm is the weak link, then ``switching to
MPC helps'' is an artifact of a weak baseline rather than a property of the
architecture}, and nothing in §3 is attributable. We built it and it holds.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Network} & \textbf{Depth} & \textbf{\baru{} CKKS} & \textbf{\heir{}} & \\
\midrule
\texttt{64x32x10}            & 3  & 181\,ms & 167\,ms & $1.08\times$ \heir{} \\
\texttt{784x32x10}           & 3  & 751\,ms & 178\,ms & $4.2\times$ \heir{} \\
\texttt{64x32x32x10}         & 5  & \textbf{384\,ms} & 659\,ms & $\mathbf{1.7\times}$ \textbf{ours} \\
\texttt{64x32x32x32x32x10}   & 9  & 1687\,ms & 1493\,ms & $1.13\times$ \heir{} \\
\texttt{64x32($\times$5)x10} & 11 & \textbf{2281\,ms} & \textbf{SIGSEGV} & \textbf{ours runs} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Same OpenFHE build \heir{} targets, same machine, same
\texttt{HEStd\_128\_classic}, and all three arms now evaluate \emph{one} fixture.
Within \textbf{13\%} at four of the five shapes \heir{} can run, faster at depth
5, and running at depth 11 where \heir{} segfaults. \texttt{784x32x10} is the one
real deficit at $4.2\times$ --- the only wide-input shape, needing 68 rotations
where same-depth \texttt{64x32x10} needs 26, so \heir{}'s shift-network passes
are doing something ours is not. That is a narrow, actionable target rather than
a vague call to improve packing, and it is Thrust~1.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Tier A also produced the sharpest evidence for the thesis so
far}, one level below where we expected it. Our CKKS arm has two dense-layer
evaluators: a direct one that rotates per output position, and an Orion-style
baby-step/giant-step transform. BSGS spends \emph{more} ciphertext
multiplications (43 to 106) to spend \emph{far fewer} rotations (337 to 26), and
it is worth $\mathbf{5.4\text{--}8.1\times}$. \textbf{It is also opt-in, behind a
flag.} So the same program, the same backend, the same security --- and an $8\times$
swing decided by a choice no cost model was making. \emph{Which evaluator is
cheapest is a property of the layer shape}, exactly as which \emph{backend} is
cheapest is a property of the program. The claim recurs at every level of the
stack we have looked at, which is the strongest argument we have that it is
structural rather than a coincidence of one comparison.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Tier B --- Expressiveness. \emph{Measured, and it cost us a
claim.}} We ran the three cases rather than citing them, and report the outcome
in full because the methodology is part of the contribution.
\textbf{(a) Exact comparison holds}: ReLU, max and argmax computed exactly
(measured; §VI.17), against which HEIR's only route is polynomial approximation
with user-annotated degree and interval --- and §VI.16 measured our own CKKS arm
being \emph{refused} at depth 3 because its logit error exceeded the class
margin, while the exact arm was right at every depth.
\textbf{(b) ``Secret-dependent control flow'' is withdrawn}: Baru lowers such an
\texttt{if} to a mux that evaluates both arms, exactly as
\texttt{convert-if-to-select} does. Obliviousness forces the same shape on both,
and we had asserted a difference that does not exist.
\textbf{(c) Mid-protocol opening holds but is narrower than claimed}: the
interpreter reveals the condition and says so, which HEIR cannot do at any
price --- but \baru{}'s own circuit path has no \texttt{reconstruct} either, so
our two backends disagree on that program.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Running Tier B also found three correctness bugs}, all in
secret-conditioned code paths no benchmark exercises: an \textbf{inverted mux}
in the MPC VM (every secret \texttt{if} took the wrong branch); a branch result
\textbf{truncated to the condition's one-bit width} (\texttt{(if (> x 0) 111
222)} returned 1); and a circuit-compiler arm that compiled \textbf{any
unsupported or misspelled call to the constant zero} without a diagnostic. All
three are fixed with both-polarity regression tests, and all 834 tests pass. No
recorded figure moved, because no benchmark program emits an \texttt{if} --- a
ReLU is \texttt{(* p (> p 0))} and an argmax is comparison bits times a product
tree --- and the only secret-conditioned \texttt{if} coverage in the tree
exercised the FHE path. That is the honest case for Tier~B existing: a feature
documented in the language reference had no coverage on two of three backends,
and a green suite said nothing about it.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Tier C --- Crossover.} Programs whose optimal assignment is
mixed: show the per-region selector finds it and both pure strategies lose. §3
already establishes the crossover exists in both directions; what remains is
showing a compiler can \emph{predict} which side a given program falls on.

\noindent\textbf{Two gates already enforced in code}, because an unfair
comparison is worse than none. \emph{(i)} A public \heir{} issue reports an MNIST
MLP at ${\sim}30$\,s / 3\,GB under OpenFHE's \texttt{HEStd\_NotSet} --- no
security level at all --- against ${\sim}6$\,min / 26\,GB at the secure ring
dimension: a $12\times$ factor between an insecure and a secure baseline. To be
exact, and fair to \heir{}: that insecure run was the reporter's choice, not a
\heir{} default --- \heir{}'s emitted C++ sets only multiplicative depth and
plaintext modulus, leaving OpenFHE's default \texttt{HEStd\_128\_classic} in
force, which we verified by reading the generated code. The gate exists because a
baseline harness must \emph{verify} the parameter it reports rather than assume
it in either direction, and ours refuses to emit a latency unless the run reports
\texttt{HEStd\_\{128,192,256\}\_classic}. \emph{(ii)} Both arms must evaluate one
fixture, with a test asserting the float evaluation reproduces the quantized
classification.

\section{Thrusts, and what the award buys}

\noindent\textbf{T1 --- Close the two packing deficits Tier~A localised}: the
wide-input layout that costs us $4.2\times$ on \texttt{784x32x10}, and
multi-channel slot packing so Tier~A extends from MLPs to CNNs. Adversarial to
our own prior numbers; we will report the shrunken figures.

\noindent\textbf{T2 --- Extending the \heir{} comparison (where the AWS
credits go).} The baseline now runs, and building it corrected our own estimate
of what it needs: we had deferred it believing secure parameters required
${\sim}26$\,GB, and the secure \texttt{784x32x10} baseline in fact peaks at
532\,MB. So the credits are \emph{not} requested for memory. Two gaps remain
that a single machine cannot close. \emph{(i)} Every wide-area figure we have,
on both arms, comes from a transport that really sleeps rather than a real link
--- and §3 shows the network profile is worth $151\times$, so this is the
largest uncontrolled variable in the whole comparison. Multi-region deployment
(\texttt{sa-east-1} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{us-east-1} $\leftrightarrow$
\texttt{eu-west-1}) replaces it with measured intercontinental RTTs.
\emph{(ii)} Separating the depth-10 cliff into ``\heir{} chose bad
parameters,'' ``OpenFHE has a bug,'' and ``this regime needs bootstrapping''
requires the larger instances where secure CKKS bootstrapping is tractable.

\noindent\textbf{T3 --- Serializable continuations and dynamic round scheduling.}
Make the continuation a value; schedule rounds dynamically by resuming whichever
subcomputation's message arrives; measure against the static scheduler it
generalises, on programs whose dependency graph is not statically known.
Secondary objective: close the interpreter overhead reported in §3.

\noindent\textbf{T4 --- The per-region backend selector}, validated against
measured runs of every assignment on small programs. The question is not whether
a cost model can be written but \emph{how coarse a model suffices}, with
``suffices'' defined by measurement.

\noindent\textbf{Deliverables.} The open-source tool; a reproducible
matched-threat-model benchmark suite spanning MPC and FHE, which the field
lacks; two publications (compiler/systems on the architecture and backend
selection; applied-crypto on switch-as-bootstrap); one doctoral thesis and two undergraduate research projects.

\section{Required questions}

\noindent\textbf{Theoretical or applied; domain.} Applied, contributing a
compiler architecture and a measurement methodology. Domain: compilers and
tooling for privacy-preserving computation, evaluated on private ML inference.

\noindent\textbf{Applications, including for Amazon.} Multi-organization
collaboration on data that cannot be pooled --- an AWS Clean Rooms-shaped
problem, and a listed topic in this call. A cost model spanning MPC and FHE
answers a provisioning question: \emph{given this workload and this network,
which privacy technology, and therefore how much compute?} §3 shows the answer
inverts with the deployment network alone. Secondarily, deciding when
cryptography beats a hardware enclave for a given program region. LGPD-driven
demand for private analytics in Latin America is served from
\texttt{sa-east-1} --- exactly the wide-area shape we measure.

\noindent\textbf{Assumptions and factors that could undermine the results.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Our FHE arm is at \heir{} parity on MLPs but not on CNNs}
($15\times$ off Orion there, 3.5\% slot utilisation), so §VI.10's CNN margins
are over our own implementation while §3's MLP results are not. T1 extends the
Tier~A footing to CNNs and will reduce our headline margins.
\item \emph{Single-machine provenance.} Wide-area latency is injected by a
sleeping transport, not a real link --- and §3 shows the profile is worth
$217\times$. T2 replaces it: the largest uncontrolled variable we have.
\item \emph{The depth/precision sweep uses an untrained fixture}, whose tight
class margins are a fixture property. The \emph{mechanism} is supported; a
quantitative accuracy claim for trained networks needs a trained fixture.
\item \emph{Threat models are matched but not identical in kind.} FHE lets the
client go offline; 2PC needs it present throughout. Matching the adversary model
does not remove this, and it may dominate for some deployments.
\item \emph{Risk to the thesis.} If a well-packed FHE arm wins in every regime
the selector degenerates and the claim weakens to expressiveness alone; the
experiment is designed so that outcome is publishable.
\item \emph{Risk to the comparison.} \heir{} is a moving target; we pin a
release, report its commit, and treat Tier~A as a control, not a scoreboard.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix A --- Measurement detail}

\textbf{Provenance.} Every figure is an elapsed wall-clock time or an

\noindent The 2PC columns are at \emph{matched disclosure} --- both arms return
the logit vector. The last column is our default, which computes the argmax
inside the protocol and reveals only the class: a guarantee \heir{} cannot offer
at any price, costing ${\approx}2.5\times$ on WAN. Single runs, ${\approx}1\%$
jitter.

\noindent Measured slopes per added hidden layer: \heir{}
${\approx}442$\,ms, ours ${\approx}222$\,ms on WAN and under 1\,ms on LAN ---
half \heir{}'s, which is why the ratio moves monotonically our way with depth.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Four things this does not say.} \emph{(i)} These are dense
MLPs, not the convolutional LeNet-5 of §A.2--A.3; the two sets compare only
within themselves. \emph{(ii)} \texttt{dealer} is a lower bound --- against
\texttt{lowgear} totals the LAN comparison is roughly a tie
(264\,ms vs.\ $167+92$\,ms), and the defensible LAN claim is the \emph{online}
one. \emph{(iii)} The interaction asymmetry is unchanged: \heir{}'s client can
go offline, ours cannot. \emph{(iv)} The disclosure asymmetry is now
measured rather than noted: see the last column.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Configuration alone was worth $2.4\text{--}3.8\times$.} The
optimized column enables truncation pairs (§VI.9) and bilinear operand fusion
(§VI.13); the base column is how \baru{}'s own \texttt{bench-2pc-nn} runs, which
enables neither. Turning them on cuts exchanges from 137 to 36 at depth 10 and
wide-area elapsed from 7695\,ms to 1999\,ms --- no new algorithm, work already
in the repository that was not reaching the comparison. The defect is
instructive: it presented not as a wrong number but as a \emph{correct} number
for a configuration nobody would deploy, which is the failure mode a
backend-aware cost model exists to prevent. A second finding from the same
re-run: \texttt{bench-2pc-nn}'s wide-area column is
\texttt{rounds}\,$\times$\,RTT\,$+$\,bytes/bandwidth\,$+$\,compute --- a cost
model, not elapsed time --- and the figures above replace it with wall clock
from two parties over a transport that really sleeps.

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent All-pairs argmax is the thesis in miniature: it performs 45
comparisons instead of 9, and is correct \emph{only} because a separate
measurement showed that workload had bandwidth headroom. On a bandwidth-bound
link the opposite lowering wins, so both are kept and selected by cost model ---
a decision a single-paradigm compiler has no basis to make.
